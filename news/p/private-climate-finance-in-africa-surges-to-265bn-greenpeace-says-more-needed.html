Private Climate Finance in Africa Surges to $26.5bn, Greenpeace Says More Needed

Private climate finance in Africa has jumped to $26.5bn, a three‑fold increase from 2023. Greenpeace International applauds the growth but stresses the need for stronger public support.

Latest developments

The latest figures released by the Climate Finance Initiative show that private climate finance in Africa has risen to $26.5bn in 2024, a three‑fold increase compared with the previous year. The report notes that 76% of the new capital was raised domestically, highlighting a growing capacity of African investors to fund green projects. The surge reflects heightened interest from institutional investors, impact funds and corporate sustainability programmes that are targeting renewable energy, sustainable agriculture and climate‑resilient infrastructure across the continent. Greenpeace International, while welcoming the momentum, cautioned that the scale of private money alone will not close the financing gap identified by the United Nations. In a recent statement, the organisation called for governments in Europe and the United Kingdom to honour pledged climate aid and to create policy frameworks that can attract even more private capital. The private sector’s enthusiasm has been echoed by other environmental groups, who see the trend as a sign that market‑based solutions are gaining traction, yet they also warn that without robust regulatory oversight, some projects could fall short of delivering real emissions reductions. The data aligns with earlier reporting by Energy News Network and Semafor, which both highlighted the rapid expansion of private investments in African climate projects during 2024.

Why it matters

For readers in Great Britain, the rise in African climate finance has direct implications for the UK’s own climate commitments. The UK government has pledged to increase its overseas development assistance for climate action, and the new funding flows from Africa could serve as a benchmark for how private capital can complement public aid. Moreover, the increased activity creates opportunities for UK‑based green finance firms to partner with African counterparts, potentially opening new markets for British investors and technology providers. The trend also influences the global carbon market, as successful projects in Africa can generate verified emission reductions that may be sold to European companies seeking to meet their net‑zero targets. Finally, the heightened visibility of climate finance in Africa may shape public opinion in the UK, encouraging voters to demand stronger climate policies and more transparent reporting on the use of public funds abroad.

Background

Africa has long faced a financing shortfall for climate mitigation and adaptation, with the United Nations estimating a need for over $1 trillion by 2030. Historically, most climate funding has come from multilateral development banks and bilateral aid, but private capital has lagged due to perceived risks and limited investment pipelines. Over the past decade, initiatives such as the African Climate‑Smart Investment Platform and the Green Climate Fund have worked to de‑risk projects and improve data availability. In 2023, private climate finance in Africa stood at roughly $8.5bn, according to the Climate Finance Initiative. The dramatic rise to $26.5bn in 2024 reflects a convergence of factors: improved regulatory environments in several African nations, the rollout of blended finance mechanisms, and a growing appetite among investors for ESG‑aligned assets. Greenpeace International has been active on the continent for years, campaigning for stronger environmental standards and supporting community‑led renewable energy projects.

What happens next

Analysts expect the upward trajectory of private climate finance to continue, provided that policy certainty remains high and that public funding does not retreat. The UK is expected to announce its next round of climate aid in early 2027, and NGOs such as Greenpeace are likely to lobby for a larger share to be earmarked for capacity‑building and project verification in Africa. In parallel, investors are watching for new financing instruments, such as green bonds issued by African sovereigns, which could further broaden the pool of capital. The coming months will also see a series of high‑level meetings, including the UN Climate Change Conference, where the role of private finance in developing regions will be a key agenda item.

Summary

- Private climate finance in Africa reached $26.5bn in 2024, a three‑fold increase from the previous year. - 76% of the new funding was raised domestically, showing stronger local investor participation. - Greenpeace International praised the growth but urged governments, especially the UK, to maintain and increase public climate aid. - The surge creates new partnership opportunities for UK green finance firms and supports global carbon‑offset markets. - Continued progress depends on stable policy frameworks, blended finance tools and sustained public‑sector support.
