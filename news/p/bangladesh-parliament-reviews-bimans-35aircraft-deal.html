Bangladesh Parliament Reviews Biman's 35‑Aircraft Deal

Bangladesh's lawmakers are probing a massive aircraft purchase by the national carrier. Experts say the deal must be matched by organisational changes.

Latest developments

A parliamentary committee has opened a formal inquiry into Biman Bangladesh Airlines' proposed acquisition of 35 new aircraft from Boeing and Airbus. The committee, citing concerns over financial viability and procurement transparency, asked senior officials to justify the scale of the deal and its alignment with the airline's current debt burden. The questioning follows a report in Ajker Patrika that highlighted doubts about the feasibility of such a large order given Biman's recent cash flow challenges.

At the same time, an editorial in The Daily Star argued that any fleet expansion must be accompanied by deep structural reform within the carrier. The piece pointed to longstanding issues such as outdated management practices, limited revenue diversification, and a need for stronger corporate governance. It warned that without these changes, the new aircraft could become a financial liability rather than an asset.

In response to growing scrutiny, the Jatiya Sangsad (JS) body announced plans to create a dedicated subcommittee tasked with overseeing both the aviation and tourism sectors. Bangladesh Sangbad Sangstha reported that the subcommittee will monitor procurement processes, evaluate performance metrics, and recommend policy adjustments to improve sectoral efficiency. The move is seen as an effort to bring greater parliamentary oversight to large‑scale projects like Biman's aircraft purchase.

Biman officials have indicated that they will provide a detailed briefing to the committee within the next two weeks. They emphasized that the procurement strategy is intended to modernise the fleet, improve route coverage, and enhance passenger experience. While no final decision has been taken, the airline has signalled willingness to cooperate with the parliamentary review and to consider recommendations that could reshape the deal.

Why it matters

For South African travelers and investors, the outcome of Bangladesh's review could influence regional air connectivity and market dynamics. A modernised Biman fleet may open new direct routes between South Asia and Africa, potentially reducing travel time and cost for business and leisure passengers. Conversely, if the deal stalls, airlines that rely on connecting traffic through Dhaka could face reduced capacity, affecting itinerary options for South African travellers heading to the Indian subcontinent.

The financial health of Biman also matters to global investors watching emerging‑market airlines. A successful restructuring and transparent procurement could attract foreign capital, while a failed or delayed deal might raise concerns about governance standards in Bangladesh's state‑owned enterprises. South African investors with exposure to aviation assets or supply‑chain partners could see their risk assessments shift based on how Bangladesh handles this high‑profile purchase.

Background

Biman Bangladesh Airlines, the country's flag carrier, was founded in 1972 shortly after independence. Over the decades, the airline has struggled with aging aircraft, limited route networks, and chronic financial deficits. Past attempts to modernise the fleet have often been hampered by funding shortfalls and delays in aircraft deliveries. In recent years, Biman has relied heavily on government subsidies to maintain operations, prompting calls for a more sustainable business model.

The current proposal to acquire 35 new jets represents the largest single procurement in the airline's history. The mix of Boeing and Airbus models is intended to replace older planes, expand long‑haul capabilities, and support new destinations. However, the scale of the order raises questions about financing, as the airline's balance sheet shows significant liabilities. Critics argue that without a clear repayment plan and governance reforms, the purchase could exacerbate existing fiscal pressures.

What happens next

The parliamentary subcommittee is expected to submit an interim report within a month, outlining any recommended adjustments to the procurement plan. If the committee finds the deal financially unsound, it may request renegotiation of terms, a reduction in the number of aircraft, or the inclusion of performance‑based clauses. Biman is likely to revise its financing strategy, possibly seeking a blend of government funding, commercial loans, and leasing arrangements to meet any new conditions.

Stakeholders, including aircraft manufacturers and financing institutions, will watch the process closely. A transparent outcome could set a precedent for future large‑scale infrastructure projects in Bangladesh, while a protracted dispute could delay fleet renewal and affect the airline's competitive position in the region.
