Historic Exmoor Clapper Bridge Destroyed by Storms Austen and Boelo

The ancient Exmoor clapper bridge was destroyed by consecutive storms Austen and Boelo. The loss has sparked calls for urgent restoration and safety reviews.

Latest developments

On 27 October, the weather‑worn clapper bridge that spans the River Exe in Exmoor National Park was swept from its foundations by the twin onslaught of Storms Austen and Boelo. The 12‑metre wooden structure, which had stood for more than 200 years, collapsed into the river, leaving only a twisted skeleton of its former form.

Meteorologists said the storms brought sustained winds of up to 80 mph and heavy rainfall that saturated the riverbanks. The sudden rise in water level, combined with the force of the gale, undermined the stone piers that had supported the bridge for generations.

Local residents and walkers who had used the bridge for daily commutes and recreational walks were left stranded. The loss of the crossing has disrupted access to the surrounding moorland and the nearby village of Lynton, forcing detours that add several miles to the journey.

The South West Coast Path Association and the Exmoor National Park Authority have issued emergency statements urging caution. Park staff are conducting a rapid assessment of the damage and coordinating with the Environment Agency to determine the safety of the surrounding area.

The clapper bridge, also known by locals as the 'tarr steps', is a rare surviving example of medieval engineering. Its destruction marks the loss of a tangible link to the region's past and raises questions about the resilience of heritage structures in the face of increasingly severe weather.

Why it matters

The bridge's loss is more than a local inconvenience; it represents a cultural loss for the entire United Kingdom. The Exmoor clapper bridge was one of only a handful of such structures remaining in Britain, and its disappearance underscores the vulnerability of historic assets to climate change.

For the tourism industry, the bridge had been a key attraction for walkers and history enthusiasts. Its absence could reduce visitor numbers to the area, affecting local businesses that rely on the steady flow of holidaymakers.

Background

Clapper bridges are simple stone or timber structures that were built in the Middle Ages to allow people and livestock to cross rivers. The Exmoor bridge, constructed in the early 19th century, was originally part of a network of routes that facilitated trade between inland communities and coastal ports.

Over the years, the bridge has survived numerous floods and storms, earning a reputation for durability. It was listed as a Grade II structure in 1985, recognising its architectural and historical importance.

What happens next

The Exmoor National Park Authority has announced that a detailed engineering survey will be commissioned within the next fortnight to assess the feasibility of rebuilding the bridge. Funding will be sought from the Heritage Lottery Fund and the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport, with a target of restoring the crossing within two years.
