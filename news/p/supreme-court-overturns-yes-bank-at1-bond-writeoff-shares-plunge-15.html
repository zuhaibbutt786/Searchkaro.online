Supreme Court Overturns Yes Bank AT1 Bond Write‑Off, Shares Plunge 15%

The Supreme Court has overturned a key ruling that forced Yes Bank to write off ₹8,400 crore of its AT1 bonds, sending the bank’s shares tumbling by 15%. Investors are scrambling to assess the impact on the bank’s capital and future debt pl

Latest developments

The Supreme Court overturned a 2023 High Court ruling that had written off ₹8,400 crore of Yes Bank’s AT1 bonds. The decision was announced on 15 Oct 2024, reversing a judgment that had forced the bank to write off a significant portion of its subordinated debt, which had already triggered a sharp decline in investor confidence. The Supreme Court held that the write‑off was not justified under the legal framework governing such instruments.

The ruling came at a time when Yes Bank’s shares were already trading below their pre‑announcement levels. Within hours of the decision, the stock fell by about 14–15%, wiping out roughly ₹1,200 crore in market value. The drop was the steepest since the bank’s 2020 crisis and sparked a wave of sell‑offs across the market.

Market analysts noted that the court’s decision removed a key regulatory hurdle, but it also raised questions about the bank’s capital structure and future debt issuances. Investors expressed concerns that the write‑off could lead to further losses if the bank’s earnings remain volatile.

In response, the bank’s management issued a statement saying it would review its capital plans and engage with shareholders. The statement also highlighted ongoing efforts to strengthen the balance sheet and restore confidence among investors.

Why it matters

For retail investors, the sudden plunge in Yes Bank’s share price means immediate losses on holdings and a reassessment of risk exposure in the banking sector. The event underscores the fragility of banks that rely heavily on subordinated debt for capital.

The ruling also signals to the broader market that the judiciary is willing to scrutinise corporate debt restructurings. It could influence how other banks structure their capital and how regulators oversee debt instruments.

Additionally, the episode may affect the pricing of other banks’ AT1 bonds, as investors reassess the legal risks associated with such securities.

Background

Yes Bank, founded in 2004, has faced a series of financial challenges over the past decade, including a liquidity crisis in 2018 that required a government‑backed recapitalisation. The 2023 High Court ruling that forced the write‑off of ₹8,400 crore in AT1 bonds was part of a broader effort to enforce stricter capital adequacy norms.

The bank’s AT1 bonds are a form of hybrid debt that can be written down if the bank’s capital falls below a certain threshold. They are attractive to investors seeking higher yields, but they carry higher risk compared to conventional bonds.

Prior to the Supreme Court decision, the bank had already been working on a capital‑raising plan that involved issuing new equity and debt to shore up its balance sheet.

What happens next

The bank is expected to file a detailed plan with regulators outlining how it will manage the write‑off and restore capital adequacy. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) may impose additional conditions on the bank’s future bond issuances.

Investors will likely monitor the bank’s quarterly reports for signs of recovery, while analysts may adjust their price targets based on the new regulatory landscape.
