India braces for heavy rains as IMD signals cyclonic activity

India's meteorological department warns of increased rainfall as cyclonic patterns develop over the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea. Southern states brace for thunderstorms and a wet Deepavali.

Latest developments

The India Meteorological Department (IMD) issued a new bulletin on Monday, indicating that the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea are showing early signs of cyclonic development. The department warns that the combination of warm sea surface temperatures and favourable wind shear could lead to enhanced rainfall over coastal and inland areas. In a separate advisory, the IMD highlighted the likelihood of widespread thunderstorms across southern India, particularly in the states of Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu. The agency noted that the convective activity is expected to intensify over the next 48 hours, with peak rainfall occurring in the late evening and overnight hours. The forecast has particular relevance for the upcoming Deepavali celebrations in South Tamil Nadu. Local authorities have issued a warning that the region could experience a wet festival, with heavy rain expected to persist through November 8. Residents are advised to prepare for potential disruptions to public transport and outdoor events. Farmers in the deltaic regions of West Bengal and the coastal districts of Andhra Pradesh have already begun to adjust irrigation schedules in anticipation of the increased precipitation. Meanwhile, the Indian Railways has announced that several train routes along the western coast may face delays due to saturated tracks and low visibility.

Why it matters

For millions of Indians, rainfall patterns directly influence food security, water availability, and daily life. The projected surge in precipitation could alleviate water shortages in drought‑prone areas but also heighten the risk of flooding in low‑lying districts. The timing of the rains coincides with the harvest season for several crops, including rice and sugarcane. Excessive moisture could damage seedlings and reduce yields, while timely rainfall can support the final growth stages of many staples. Urban centres in the south are also preparing for heavy downpours. City authorities have activated drainage protocols and are monitoring river levels to mitigate the risk of flash floods, especially in densely populated neighbourhoods.

Background

India's monsoon system is governed by the complex interaction between the Indian Ocean and the Indian subcontinent. The Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea act as heat reservoirs, and their temperatures influence the development of low‑pressure zones that can evolve into tropical cyclones. The IMD, established in 1875, is the national authority responsible for forecasting weather and issuing warnings for cyclonic activity. Its latest bulletin reflects a growing trend of cyclogenesis in the Indian Ocean during late autumn, a phenomenon linked to broader climate change patterns. Historically, the southern states have experienced a mix of thunderstorms and monsoon rains during November. However, the intensity and frequency of such events have increased in recent years, prompting the department to issue more frequent advisories.

What happens next

The IMD will continue to monitor sea surface temperatures and wind patterns over the next week, issuing updated warnings as conditions evolve. Residents in affected regions are urged to stay informed through local radio, television, and the department’s official website. If the cyclonic signs intensify, the department may issue a cyclone alert, triggering emergency response protocols across coastal districts. The government has already mobilised resources to reinforce embankments and stockpile relief supplies.
