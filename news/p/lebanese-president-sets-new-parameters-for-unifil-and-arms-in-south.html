Lebanese President Sets New Parameters for UNIFIL and Arms in South

The Lebanese president has outlined a new approach to the United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL) and to weapons restrictions in the south. The move is expected to influence security dynamics across the region.

Latest developments

In a meeting held on Thursday, Lebanese President Michel Aoun discussed the future of "اليونيفيل" (UNIFIL) with the United Nations deputy secretary‑general. The talks focused on adjusting the force's mandate in southern Lebanon after a series of cross‑border incidents. Aoun indicated that any extension of UNIFIL’s presence would be tied to a broader security framework that includes a calibrated reduction of locally held arms. He emphasized that the decision would reflect a balance of security, political, social and economic considerations, signalling a shift from the status quo that has persisted since the 2006 war. The president also hinted at a possible review of the 1978 and 1982 UN resolutions that established the force, pending further consultations with regional partners and the Lebanese parliament.

Why it matters

The announcement arrives at a time when Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states are closely monitoring Lebanon's internal security calculations. A more limited UNIFIL footprint could alter the power balance between the Lebanese armed forces and non‑state actors, notably Hezbollah, which has long used the southern border as a strategic depth. For Saudi investors and expatriates, stability in Lebanon translates into reduced risk for trade routes and energy projects that cross the Levant corridor. Moreover, the arms‑restriction component may affect illicit smuggling networks that have historically thrived in the border area, potentially curbing revenue streams that fund militant groups. Regional observers see the decision as a test of Lebanon's ability to assert sovereignty while still relying on international peacekeepers for deterrence.

Background

UNIFIL was created in 1978 following Israel's invasion of southern Lebanon, with a mandate to confirm Israeli withdrawal and assist the Lebanese government in restoring its authority. The force was later expanded after the 2006 conflict, operating alongside the Lebanese Armed Forces (LAF) to monitor the Blue Line and prevent hostilities. Over the years, UNIFIL's presence has been both praised for reducing large‑scale clashes and criticized for limiting Lebanon's own security initiatives. Parallel to the UN mission, Lebanon has grappled with a proliferation of weapons among civilian populations and armed groups, a legacy of the civil war and subsequent conflicts. President Aoun's recent remarks echo earlier calls for a comprehensive disarmament strategy that aligns with the country's economic recovery plans.

What happens next

The president’s statements are expected to be forwarded to the Lebanese cabinet for formal approval, after which a draft resolution will be presented to the United Nations Security Council. International diplomats will likely seek assurances that any scaling back of UNIFIL will not create a security vacuum. In parallel, the Lebanese parliament is slated to debate a new arms‑control bill that would codify the security, political, social and economic criteria outlined by Aoun. Observers anticipate that the UN will conduct a feasibility study before any mandate adjustment, while neighboring countries may issue statements to clarify their positions on cross‑border security cooperation.
