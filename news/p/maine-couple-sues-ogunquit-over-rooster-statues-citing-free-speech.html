Maine Couple Sues Ogunquit Over Rooster Statues, Citing Free Speech

A family and an omelette factory in Ogunquit are challenging the town's classification of decorative rooster statues as illegal advertising. The cases raise questions about free speech and municipal authority.

Latest developments

A family from Ogunquit, Maine, has filed a federal lawsuit against the town after officials declared the rooster statues they placed on their property to be illegal "advertising signs." The plaintiffs argue that the town's decision infringes on their First Amendment rights to expressive conduct. The case was filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Maine and seeks an injunction that would allow the family to keep the statues.

In a related action, the owners of a local omelette factory, which also displays rooster-themed decorations as part of its branding, have joined the legal battle. The business alleges that the town's enforcement actions have disrupted its marketing efforts and caused financial harm. Both lawsuits cite recent statements from town officials that the statues violate municipal zoning rules intended to prevent commercial advertising in residential areas.

Town officials maintain that the statues are not merely decorative but serve as promotional material for the family's private business and the omelette factory. They contend that the zoning ordinance was adopted to preserve the residential character of the neighborhood and to prevent visual clutter. The town has not yet responded to requests for comment on the lawsuits.

Legal analysts note that the cases could set a precedent for how municipalities treat decorative objects that also function as branding tools. The plaintiffs have retained counsel from the Institute for Justice, a nonprofit organization that frequently challenges government restrictions on speech. The court is expected to schedule a hearing on the preliminary injunction request within the next six weeks.

Why it matters

The lawsuits highlight a growing tension between local zoning authority and individual expression. If the courts side with the plaintiffs, municipalities may need to revise ordinances that broadly define "advertising" to avoid infringing on protected speech.

For residents across the United States, the outcome could affect how homeowners display art, symbols, or thematic decorations that have a commercial undertone. The case also underscores the role of nonprofit legal groups in supporting First Amendment challenges, potentially encouraging similar actions in other jurisdictions.

Background

Ogunquit, a coastal town in southern Maine, has a history of strict zoning enforcement aimed at preserving its historic and residential character. In recent years, the town has increased scrutiny of outdoor displays that it believes could be interpreted as commercial signage. The rooster statues, chosen by the family as a whimsical nod to their poultry-themed business, were installed on their front lawn in 2024.

The family, which includes an autistic daughter who enjoys the statues, argues that the decorations are a form of personal expression and not a commercial advertisement. The town's interpretation stems from a 2022 ordinance that requires any object exceeding a certain size and bearing a brand name to obtain a permit. The ordinance has been applied to a range of items, from painted fences to garden gnomes with logos.

What happens next

The district court will first consider whether to grant a temporary restraining order that would allow the statues to remain while the case proceeds. Both sides are expected to file extensive briefs outlining the legal standards for protected speech in zoning contexts.

If the court rules in favor of the plaintiffs, the town may be compelled to amend its ordinance or adopt a more narrowly tailored definition of advertising. Conversely, a ruling for the town could reinforce municipal authority to regulate visual elements in residential neighborhoods.
