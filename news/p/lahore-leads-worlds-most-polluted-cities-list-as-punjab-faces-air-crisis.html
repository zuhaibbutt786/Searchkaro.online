Lahore Leads World’s Most Polluted Cities List as Punjab Faces Air Crisis

Latest developments

According to Dawn, Lahore has been placed at the top of a global ranking of major cities with the highest concentrations of fine particulate matter (PM2.5). The city recorded an annual average of over 150 micrograms per cubic metre, a level more than three times the World Health Organization’s safe limit. The ranking, compiled from satellite data and ground monitors, places Lahore ahead of other South Asian metropolises and underscores a rapid deterioration in air quality during the winter heating season.

Mongabay reports that the deteriorating air in Punjab is not only a human health issue but also a growing threat to wildlife. Researchers highlighted a monitoring blind spot in the province’s rural zones, where limited air‑quality stations fail to capture pollutant exposure for birds, mammals and insects. Preliminary observations suggest that elevated ozone and particulate levels are affecting breeding success and foraging behavior of several species, raising concerns among conservationists about long‑term ecosystem impacts.

The News Pakistan notes that the Punjab Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has taken a concrete step by demolishing 197 brick kilns identified as major sources of smog. The operation, part of an anti‑smog crackdown launched earlier this year, targeted kilns that were operating without emission controls and using outdated technology. Officials said the removal of these kilns could cut regional particulate emissions by an estimated 10 percent, though they warned that enforcement must continue to achieve lasting improvement.

Provincial authorities have also announced additional measures, including the expansion of air‑quality monitoring networks and the promotion of cleaner fuel alternatives for households. The Punjab government plans to install 150 new monitoring stations by the end of 2027, aiming to fill the data gaps highlighted by recent studies. Together, the demolition of polluting kilns and the enhanced monitoring are intended to curb the smog episodes that have become a seasonal norm in the region.

Why it matters

Poor air quality in Punjab directly affects the health of millions of residents. Elevated levels of PM2.5 and nitrogen dioxide are linked to increased rates of asthma, chronic bronchitis and cardiovascular disease, especially among children, the elderly and outdoor workers. Hospitals in Lahore have reported a surge in respiratory admissions during peak smog weeks, placing additional strain on an already stretched health system.

The pollution also carries economic consequences. Frequent smog episodes reduce outdoor labor productivity, disrupt school attendance and deter tourism, which together cost the provincial economy billions of rupees each year. Moreover, the threat to wildlife can undermine agricultural pollination services and biodiversity‑dependent livelihoods, amplifying the financial burden on rural communities.

Airborne pollutants do not respect provincial borders; the haze from Punjab drifts into neighboring Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and even across the border into India’s northern states. This trans‑regional impact highlights the need for coordinated policy responses and aligns with broader South Asian efforts to meet the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 3 on health and well‑being.

Background

Air pollution has been a growing challenge in Pakistan since the early 2000s, driven by rapid urbanisation, expanding vehicle fleets and the widespread use of low‑efficiency coal and wood for heating and industrial processes. Brick kilns, which number in the tens of thousands nationwide, have long been identified as major emitters of particulate matter and sulfur oxides, contributing significantly to the country’s overall emissions profile.

Efforts to address the problem have been fragmented. While the federal government introduced the National Air Quality Standards in 2015, implementation has been uneven, and many districts lack functional monitoring stations. In Punjab, a 2019 initiative to upgrade kiln technology saw limited uptake, and a 2021 study highlighted a ‘monitoring blind spot’ in rural areas where data collection remains sparse.

Internationally, cities such as Delhi, Beijing and Tehran have faced similar smog crises and have implemented large‑scale measures ranging from temporary vehicle bans to the conversion of kilns to cleaner fuels. The World Health Organization recommends an annual PM2.5 average of no more than 10 micrograms per cubic metre, a benchmark that Lahore’s current levels far exceed.

What happens next

Environmental officials say the demolition of the 197 kilns is only the first phase of a broader anti‑smog strategy. The EPA plans to audit remaining kilns and issue compliance notices, with non‑conforming operations facing fines or shutdowns. Simultaneously, the provincial government is set to launch a public awareness campaign encouraging the use of clean cooking stoves and promoting tree‑planting initiatives in urban districts.

If these measures are sustained, air‑quality experts project that Lahore’s PM2.5 levels could fall by 20 to 30 percent within the next three years. However, success will depend on consistent enforcement, the expansion of monitoring infrastructure and the willingness of industry stakeholders to adopt greener technologies.
