Cyclone Arnab Expected to Hit Andhra Pradesh Coast on Oct 28

A deep depression over the Bay of Bengal is set to intensify into Cyclone Arnab and strike Andhra Pradesh on Oct 28. Authorities have declared a high alert and begun evacuations.

Latest developments

The Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) announced on October 26 that a deep depression over the Bay of Bengal is likely to intensify into a very severe cyclonic storm, officially named Arnab. Forecast models show the system moving west‑northwest at a steady pace and making landfall along the Andhra Pradesh coastline on October 28. The IMD expects sustained wind speeds of 120 to 140 km/h at landfall, with gusts possibly exceeding 160 km/h. Heavy rainfall is projected for three consecutive days, raising the risk of flash floods and landslides in low‑lying areas.

Satellite imagery released by the IMD shows rapid organization of the system, with a well‑defined eye forming and central pressure dropping sharply. The storm's convection has expanded, covering a radius of about 250 km. Meteorologists warn that the storm could strengthen further before reaching the coast, as sea‑surface temperatures remain above 30°C, providing ample energy for intensification. The IMD has issued a cyclone warning for the entire stretch of the Andhra coast, from Srikakulam in the north to Nellore in the south.

In response, the Andhra Pradesh state government has declared a high alert for the coastal districts. Disaster response teams from the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) have been deployed, and over 150,000 residents in vulnerable villages have been moved to temporary shelters. Power utilities are pre‑positioning repair crews, and the state’s health department has stocked essential medicines and clean water supplies. Local authorities are also coordinating with the Indian Navy to use ships for evacuation and relief operations if needed.

While the primary threat is to Andhra Pradesh, the broader Bay of Bengal region, including parts of West Bengal and Odisha, is experiencing a sharp drop in atmospheric pressure and strong winds. Coastal fishermen have been advised to remain ashore, and ports along the eastern seaboard have temporarily halted cargo operations as a precaution.

Why it matters

Andhra Pradesh is home to more than 50 million people, many of whom depend on agriculture and fishing for their livelihoods. The projected three‑day deluge could inundate rice paddies, damage sugarcane fields, and disrupt the harvest cycle, leading to short‑term food supply challenges and potential price spikes. Infrastructure such as roads, bridges, and power lines in coastal districts is vulnerable to wind damage, which could isolate communities and delay relief efforts.

The cyclone also poses a public health risk. Stagnant water can become a breeding ground for mosquitoes, increasing the likelihood of vector‑borne diseases such as dengue and malaria. Additionally, the displacement of large numbers of residents raises concerns about adequate shelter, sanitation, and access to medical care. The high alert status reflects the government's effort to mitigate these risks, but the scale of the storm means that emergency services will be stretched.

Economically, the region contributes significantly to India's industrial output, with several ports and manufacturing hubs located near the coast. Any prolonged disruption could affect supply chains, especially for commodities exported through Visakhapatnam and Kakinada. Insurance claims from property damage are likely to rise, putting pressure on both private insurers and government relief funds.

Background

Cyclones in the Indian subcontinent typically develop in the warm waters of the Bay of Bengal between April and December, with peak activity in October and November. The region's geography—shallow continental shelf and high sea‑surface temperatures—creates ideal conditions for rapid storm formation. The naming system for cyclones in the North Indian Ocean is managed by the World Meteorological Organization, with member countries contributing names in a rotating list. "Arnab" was submitted by Pakistan and means "lion" in Urdu.

The Kannada term "ಚಂಡಮಾರುತ" (pronounced chandaamaaruta) translates directly to "cyclone" in English. It is commonly used in Karnataka media to describe these intense weather systems. Over the past decade, the Bay of Bengal has produced several notable cyclones, including Cyclone Phailin in 2013 and Cyclone Hudhud in 2014, both of which caused extensive damage to coastal communities. Lessons learned from those events have led to improved early warning systems and evacuation protocols, which are now being applied to the current threat.

Climate scientists have noted an upward trend in the frequency of very severe cyclonic storms in the region, attributing part of this rise to rising ocean temperatures linked to global warming. While individual storms cannot be directly blamed on climate change, the overall pattern suggests that future cyclones may become more intense and pose greater challenges for disaster management.

What happens next

The IMD will continue to issue updates every six hours as Cyclone Arnab approaches the coastline. Residents in the designated high‑risk zones are advised to stay in designated shelters, keep emergency kits ready, and follow instructions from local authorities. After landfall, the focus will shift to rescue operations, clearing of debris, and restoration of essential services. The state government has pledged financial assistance for affected families and has requested additional support from the central disaster fund.

In the days following the storm, damage assessments will be carried out by the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) to determine the extent of infrastructure loss and to coordinate long‑term rehabilitation efforts. International aid agencies have expressed readiness to provide relief supplies if the situation escalates.
