U.S. Daylight Saving Time Ends Oct 31, 2026 as States Debate Permanent Shift

U.S. clocks will turn back on Oct. 31, 2026, ending daylight saving time for the first time in decades. Some states are pushing for a permanent daylight saving schedule.

Latest developments

The United States will cease observing daylight saving time on October 31, 2026, when clocks will fall back one hour at 2 a.m. local time. For the first time in decades, the nation will return to standard time permanently, ending a practice that has been in place since the 2005 Energy Policy Act extended the period of daylight saving to 2007.

State lawmakers are divided over whether to adopt a permanent daylight saving schedule or a permanent standard time. Several states have already passed legislation to keep the clocks permanently ahead, citing benefits such as increased evening daylight for commerce and recreation. Others have pushed for a permanent return to standard time to avoid the biannual clock changes.

The holiday of Halloween will fall on the day the clocks change, meaning that the evening will begin at 10 p.m. local time instead of 9 p.m. for the first time in more than a century. The change is expected to cause confusion for children, parents, and event organizers, and it will be the last time the holiday coincides with a daylight saving transition for many years.

Federal law currently allows states to choose permanent daylight saving or standard time only if Congress passes a new act. While no such legislation has yet been enacted, lawmakers in Washington are monitoring the situation closely, and several bills have been introduced to formalize the choice that states will make in 2026.

Why it matters

The shift will affect daily routines, as people will have to adjust to an earlier sunrise and sunset. Studies have linked the biannual clock changes to disruptions in sleep patterns, increased risk of heart attacks, and higher rates of traffic accidents. The 2026 change will remove this disruption, but the transition to standard time may still cause short‑term adjustment issues.

From an economic perspective, the end of daylight saving could influence energy consumption, retail sales, and tourism. Proponents argue that permanent daylight saving would reduce energy use by decreasing the need for artificial lighting in the evenings, while opponents warn that the extra morning light could increase heating costs in colder regions.

Regulatory bodies will need to update schedules for federal agencies, schools, and transportation services. Airlines, railroads, and shipping companies will have to adjust timetables, and federal courts will change their operating hours to reflect the new time standard.

Background

Daylight saving time was first introduced in the United States during World War I as a way to conserve coal. It was reintroduced in 1965 and made permanent nationwide by the Uniform Time Act of 1966, which required all states to observe the same daylight saving schedule.

The Energy Policy Act of 2005 extended daylight saving time by four weeks, beginning in 2007, in an effort to reduce energy consumption. The act also gave states the option to adopt permanent daylight saving if they chose to do so, but no state has yet exercised that option.

Over the years, the practice has been controversial. Critics point to health risks, safety concerns, and the inconvenience of changing clocks twice a year, while supporters highlight the economic benefits and the appeal of longer evenings for leisure activities.

What happens next

In the coming months, Congress is expected to consider bills that would allow states to make a permanent choice between daylight saving and standard time. If passed, the legislation would formalize the decision that states have been making informally since 2005.

Public opinion remains divided. A recent poll found that 55% of Americans support permanent daylight saving, while 40% favor permanent standard time. State governments will likely continue to lobby for the option that best suits their economic and social interests.
