Iranian Guards say they hit US radar in Bahrain as US shoots down drones

US Central Command reported shooting down two hostile drones over Bahrain, while Iran’s Revolutionary Guard announced it had struck a US early‑warning radar on the island. The conflicting claims raise concerns about a possible escalation in

Latest developments

On 13 November 2026, United States Central Command announced that its forces had intercepted and shot down two unmanned aerial vehicles that entered Bahrain's airspace. The drones, which the US described as hostile, were neutralised before they could reach any critical infrastructure. At the same time, the US rejected claims that it had been hit by an Iranian missile or that its early‑warning radar on the island had been destroyed. The statements came as Tehran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) released its own account of the incident.

US officials emphasized that the drones were engaged under existing rules of engagement that protect Bahrain’s sovereign airspace, a key ally in the Gulf. They added that the early‑warning radar, part of the United States’ Ballistic Missile Early Warning System, remains fully operational and that no damage has been confirmed. The US also warned that any future attempts to target its assets would be met with a proportionate response, reiterating its commitment to regional security.

The IRGC, speaking through its media outlet Tasnim, claimed that its forces had successfully struck the US radar installation, describing the operation as a “defensive action” against what it called an “aggressive US presence.” According to the Iranian statement, the strike was carried out by a squadron of armed drones launched from Iranian territory, and the radar was rendered inoperable. The IRGC did not provide independent verification, and satellite imagery of the site has not been released.

Regional governments reacted cautiously. Saudi Arabia’s foreign ministry called for restraint and urged all parties to avoid escalation that could destabilise the Gulf. The United Arab Emirates echoed similar concerns, highlighting the potential impact on commercial aviation and oil shipments that pass through Bahrain’s airspace. Washington’s allies in Europe, including the United Kingdom, expressed support for the US position, while also urging diplomatic channels to de‑escalate the situation.

Why it matters

For readers in Pakistan, the incident matters because the Gulf’s stability directly influences oil prices, which affect the Pakistani economy. Bahrain hosts a major hub for regional finance and shipping, and any disruption could ripple through trade routes that Pakistan relies on for energy imports. Moreover, a sizable Pakistani expatriate community works in Bahrain, meaning that heightened security alerts could impact families back home.

The episode also highlights the broader US‑Iran rivalry that has shaped South Asian foreign policy. Islamabad has balanced its ties with both Washington and Tehran, and a sudden flare‑up could force Pakistan to reassess its diplomatic posture, especially in matters of counter‑terrorism cooperation and regional security frameworks.

Background

The United States has maintained an early‑warning radar installation in Bahrain since the early 2000s as part of its Ballistic Missile Early Warning System, providing detection of potential missile launches across the Middle East. Bahrain’s strategic location at the mouth of the Persian Gulf makes it a key node for US naval and air operations, and the radar is considered a critical component of the alliance’s defensive architecture.

Iran has increasingly employed armed drones to project power beyond its borders, a tactic seen in recent attacks on shipping and oil facilities. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, which controls Iran’s missile and drone programs, views US military assets in the Gulf as legitimate targets, especially after a series of sanctions and confrontations that have heightened mutual suspicion.

What happens next

US officials are expected to convene a senior‑level meeting with Gulf partners to coordinate a response and to verify the operational status of the radar. Diplomatic channels between Washington and Tehran are likely to be activated to prevent further incidents, while regional states may call for a UN‑mediated dialogue to reduce the risk of accidental escalation.
