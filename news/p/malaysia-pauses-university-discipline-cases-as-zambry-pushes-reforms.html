Malaysia pauses university discipline cases as Zambry pushes reforms

Education Minister Zambry Abdul Kadir announced a pause on disciplinary actions in public universities and a plan to unify campus rules. The move also includes the gradual repeal of the controversial AUKU legislation.

Latest developments

On 9 October 2026 the Malaysian government announced a temporary halt to disciplinary proceedings in public universities that relate to free speech, citing concerns about academic freedom. The decision was communicated by Education Minister Zambry Abdul Kadir, who said the move gives institutions time to review their policies. The pause follows a broader ministry initiative to standardise disciplinary rules across all public universities. The Ministry of Higher Education released a draft framework that will replace the patchwork of campus‑specific codes with a uniform set of procedures, eliminating the "here can, there cannot" situation that has caused confusion among students and staff. At the same time the government confirmed that the Anti‑University‑Killing (AUKU) legislation, introduced in 2022 to curb campus protests, will be phased out systematically. Zambry indicated that the law has been criticised for being overly punitive and for stifling legitimate dissent. The combined reforms are expected to be rolled out in stages, with the disciplinary halt remaining in place for at least six months while the standardised rulebook is drafted. Universities have been asked to submit their existing codes for review by the end of the year. Critics, including student unions and civil‑society groups, warn that the pause could be used to delay accountability for genuine misconduct and have called for transparent timelines and independent oversight.

Why it matters

For South African students studying in Malaysian institutions or considering Malaysia as a study destination, the reforms could affect campus climate, freedom of expression and the handling of misconduct cases. A uniform disciplinary framework may also influence bilateral education agreements, as South African universities often partner with Malaysian counterparts; clear and consistent rules could make joint programmes more attractive. The removal of AUKU legislation signals a shift toward a more open academic environment, aligning with global trends and potentially encouraging more South African scholars to collaborate with Malaysian peers.

Background

Zambry Abdul Kadir, a veteran UMNO politician, served as Perak’s chief minister before being appointed Education Minister in 2022. His tenure has been marked by attempts to modernise the education system and address student unrest. The controversy over university discipline began in 2024 when several campuses imposed sanctions on students for expressing political views online, leading to legal challenges that argued the sanctions violated constitutional rights. The AUKU law was passed in 2022 after a series of campus protests that turned violent; it gave university authorities broad powers to suspend students and staff but was condemned by human‑rights organisations as draconian.
