Labour rent reforms give councils new powers over empty homes

UK councils can now intervene in long‑empty rental properties under the latest Labour rent reforms. Landlords warn the changes could lead to loss of homes while officials stress the process is not automatic.

Latest developments

The Labour government has introduced a set of rent reforms that give local authorities the power to acquire privately owned rental properties that have remained empty for a prolonged period. Under the new rules, a council may apply for a compulsory purchase order if a dwelling has been vacant for more than two years and the owner has not taken reasonable steps to let it. The policy is intended to address the growing number of long‑term empty homes that contribute to the national housing shortage. Landlords have warned that the measures could force them to lose properties they rely on for income. Industry groups argue that the threat of compulsory acquisition creates uncertainty, potentially discouraging investment in the buy‑to‑let market. Some owners fear that even homes that are temporarily out of rent could be targeted if councils deem them to be under‑utilised. Property118 notes that a council takeover is not automatic. Authorities must first serve a notice, demonstrate that the property has been empty for the required period, and follow a legal process that includes a public inquiry if the owner objects. The compulsory purchase order can be challenged in the courts, and the council must compensate the owner at market value. In Barnsley, the local council has issued a public plea for owners of empty houses to bring them back into use, warning that prolonged vacancy harms the community. The Barnsley Chronicle report highlights that the council is prepared to use the new powers, but it also stresses the importance of cooperation between landlords and authorities to avoid forced sales.

Why it matters

The reforms could have a direct impact on the rental market by reducing the supply of buy‑to‑let units. With fewer properties available, rents may rise in areas where demand already outstrips supply, affecting tenants who are already facing cost‑of‑living pressures. For landlords, the risk of compulsory acquisition may lead some to sell existing holdings or to avoid purchasing new rental properties altogether. A contraction in private rental stock could shift more pressure onto the social housing sector, increasing the burden on council budgets. Councils, on the other hand, see the ability to convert empty homes into affordable housing as a tool to meet local targets. By bringing vacant dwellings back into use, they hope to alleviate neighbourhood blight and generate additional revenue through council‑owned rentals.

Background

The issue of empty homes has been a persistent problem in the UK, with recent estimates suggesting that more than 200,000 residential properties remain unoccupied for over a year. Critics have long argued that these homes represent a missed opportunity to ease the housing crisis. Previous government initiatives focused on tax measures such as the higher rates of council tax on empty dwellings, but they relied on owners to voluntarily bring properties back into use. The Labour reforms mark a shift toward direct intervention, building on earlier powers that allowed councils to take over derelict commercial premises. Historically, compulsory purchase has been used for infrastructure projects, but its extension to residential rentals is relatively new. Legal scholars note that the new provisions must balance public interest with property rights, a tension that has shaped housing policy debates for decades.

What happens next

The legislation is slated to come into force in early 2025, giving councils a twelve‑month window to develop the necessary administrative procedures. Guidance documents are expected from the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government in the coming months. Industry bodies have signalled that they will monitor the rollout closely and may pursue legal challenges where they believe the process breaches existing property law. Meanwhile, councils are preparing to identify candidate properties and to engage with owners before issuing compulsory purchase notices.

Summary

- Labour rent reforms grant councils power to acquire long‑empty rental homes. - Landlords warn the measures could lead to loss of investment properties. - Council takeovers require legal notices, public inquiries and compensation. - Local authorities, such as Barnsley Council, are urging owners to act voluntarily. - The changes aim to increase housing supply but may raise rents and spark legal disputes.
