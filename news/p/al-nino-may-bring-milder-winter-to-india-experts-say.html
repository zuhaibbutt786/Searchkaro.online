Al Niño may bring milder winter to India, experts say

Meteorologists say the emerging El Niño pattern could suppress typical winter cold across much of India. The shift may have immediate consequences for farming, power consumption and public health.

Latest developments

The Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) has issued an early outlook indicating that the current El Niño (Al Niño in Gujarati) event is likely to temper the country’s winter temperatures. Data from satellite observations and sea‑surface temperature analyses show a persistent warm anomaly in the central Pacific, a hallmark of El Niño, which is expected to influence the Indian monsoon trough and reduce the influx of cold air from the north. In recent weeks, several northern states reported daytime highs that are 2‑4 °C above the seasonal average, while nighttime lows have remained well above the typical frost line.

Broadcasts by BBC Gujarati highlighted the possibility that many regions may experience a winter without the usual chill. The network’s weather segment featured climatologists explaining that the warm oceanic conditions can weaken the western disturbances that normally bring cold spells to the sub‑continent. As a result, agricultural zones that rely on mild winter temperatures for certain crops could see altered growth cycles. The IMD has advised farmers to monitor sowing schedules for winter pulses and oilseeds, as delayed cooling may affect germination rates.

The Ministry of Power also noted a potential shift in electricity demand patterns. Historically, winter evenings see a surge in heating-related consumption, especially in the Himalayan foothills. A milder winter could reduce this peak, easing pressure on the grid during the post‑monsoon season. However, officials cautioned that any reduction in heating demand might be offset by increased use of cooling appliances if temperatures remain higher than usual during the day.

Public health officials are preparing for a different set of challenges. While a warmer winter may lower the incidence of cold‑related respiratory illnesses, it could also exacerbate air‑quality concerns in urban centers where temperature inversions are less likely to disperse pollutants. The combined effect of these factors is prompting a coordinated response across meteorological, agricultural and energy agencies.

Why it matters

For millions of Indians, winter weather directly influences daily routines, from heating homes to planning agricultural activities. A milder winter could reduce heating costs for households in the north, providing short‑term financial relief. Conversely, farmers who depend on a predictable cold period for crops such as wheat and mustard may need to adjust planting dates, potentially affecting yields and market prices.

Energy planners are also watching the trend closely. The national grid’s load forecasts are calibrated around seasonal demand spikes. A shift in winter temperature patterns could lead to a recalibration of supply contracts and affect the timing of new power plant commissions. Moreover, the health sector must adapt to a different disease profile, with a possible decline in flu cases but a rise in heat‑related ailments if daytime temperatures stay elevated.

Overall, the El Niño influence underscores the interconnected nature of climate, economy and public welfare in India. Policymakers are urged to incorporate the latest forecasts into sectoral planning to mitigate adverse outcomes and capitalize on any benefits.

Background

El Niño, known locally as Al Niño, is a periodic warming of sea‑surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific Ocean. The phenomenon typically recurs every two to seven years and can disrupt weather patterns worldwide. In India, El Niño has historically been linked to weaker monsoon rains, but its impact on winter conditions is less frequently discussed.

Scientific studies indicate that El Niño can alter the jet stream and reduce the frequency of western disturbances—low‑pressure systems that travel from the Mediterranean region into the Indian subcontinent. These disturbances are the primary drivers of winter rainfall and cold spells in northern India. When El Niño is strong, the jet stream tends to shift northward, limiting the reach of these systems and resulting in warmer, drier winter weather.

The current El Niño event began developing in mid‑2023, with sea‑surface temperatures rising above the 0.5 °C threshold used by climatologists to define a full‑scale event. The phenomenon is now classified as a moderate to strong El Niño, prompting heightened monitoring by the IMD and international weather agencies.

What happens next

The IMD will release weekly updates on the progression of the El Niño and its projected impact on India’s winter climate. Agricultural advisories are expected to be issued within the next fortnight, focusing on sowing windows for winter crops. Energy regulators will likely adjust demand forecasts for the upcoming months, while health ministries may issue guidance on managing air‑quality risks associated with higher temperatures.

If the El Niño persists into the early months of 2024, the country could experience an extended period of milder winter conditions, prompting a reassessment of seasonal strategies across multiple sectors.

Summary
