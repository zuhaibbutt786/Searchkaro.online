Egyptian Waqf Director Calls for Parental Role in Friday Sermon

Religious officials in Egypt used the Friday sermon to stress the importance of parents as role models and to discuss child rights under Islam. The message resonated with audiences in Saudi Arabia, where the sermon is closely followed.

Latest developments

On November 20, 2026, the director of the Eastern Province Waqf in Egypt addressed the weekly Friday sermon (خطبة الجمعة) with a focus on family education. He argued that raising children begins with parents setting a positive example, a point he illustrated with references to Quranic verses and Hadith. At the same time, another prominent religious platform, Dar Al-Hilal, dedicated its Friday sermon to the topic of "Child Rights in Islam," emphasizing legal and moral protections afforded to minors in Islamic law. The sermon was broadcast live on the first channel of Al-Masry Al-Youm, allowing millions of viewers in Saudi Arabia and the wider Arab region to watch the proceedings in real time. The live stream attracted a surge in online discussion, with social media users sharing excerpts and debating the practical implications of the messages delivered.

Why it matters

Friday sermons hold a unique place in the daily lives of Muslims across the Middle East, often shaping public opinion on social and moral issues. By highlighting parental responsibility and child rights, the Egyptian religious leaders are addressing concerns that have surfaced in recent years, such as child neglect, education gaps, and the need for stronger family values. In Saudi Arabia, where the sermon is also a key source of guidance, the themes resonated with parents and educators who are seeking clearer religious endorsement for protective measures. The emphasis on Islamic principles provides a cultural framework that can influence policy discussions, school curricula, and community programs aimed at safeguarding children.

Background

The Friday sermon, known in Arabic as خطبة الجمعة, is delivered by the imam before the congregational prayer each week. Historically, the sermon has covered a range of topics from spiritual reflection to social justice. In recent decades, many Arab governments have encouraged imams to address contemporary challenges, including health, education, and family welfare. The Waqf system, which manages religious endowments, often coordinates the content of sermons in Egypt's governorates. Meanwhile, Dar Al-Hilal, a well‑established Islamic publishing house, frequently partners with mosques to disseminate thematic sermons that align with scholarly interpretations of Sharia. The live broadcast by Al-Masry Al-Youm reflects a growing trend of digitising religious services to reach broader audiences.

What happens next

Analysts expect that the themes of parental role‑modeling and child rights will continue to appear in upcoming sermons across the region, especially as governments and religious authorities respond to public demand for clearer guidance on family matters. In Saudi Arabia, the Ministry of Islamic Affairs may issue recommendations to imams to incorporate similar messages, potentially leading to coordinated campaigns in schools and community centres. Observers will watch for any legislative proposals that reference the religious discourse, particularly those aimed at strengthening child protection laws.
