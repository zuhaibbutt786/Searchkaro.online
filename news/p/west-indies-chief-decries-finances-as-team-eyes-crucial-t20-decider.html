West Indies chief decries finances as team eyes crucial T20 decider

The West Indies cricket board says matches against India are not profitable and slams ICC revenue sharing. Meanwhile, a bowler credits slower deliveries for a surprise win in Hyderabad as the teams prepare for the fifth T20.

Latest developments

The West Indies Cricket Board (WICB) chief executive publicly stated that playing against India does not generate any profit for the Caribbean side, accusing the International Cricket Council (ICC) of an unfair revenue model. The comment came after the ongoing five‑match T20 International series, in which India currently leads the series but the West Indies managed a notable victory in Hyderabad. In that match, fast‑bowler Keemo Paul revealed that a deliberate change in pace – using slower balls – was the decisive factor that troubled the Indian batting line‑up. Paul explained that the variation disrupted timing and forced errors, allowing the West Indies to clinch a rare win on Indian soil.

As the series moves to its final encounter in Bengaluru, the WICB is weighing changes to the playing XI. Local media reports suggest two alterations may be made to strengthen the middle order and add a second pace option. The board’s financial concerns have intensified calls for a stronger performance, hoping a win in the fifth match could boost ticket sales and broadcast interest. The CEO’s remarks have also sparked a broader debate within the cricket community about how ICC revenue is allocated among full‑member nations, with smaller boards demanding a more equitable share.

The upcoming match is expected to feature a blend of experienced campaigners and emerging talent, as the West Indies aim to end the series on a high note despite the financial pressures highlighted by their chief executive.

Why it matters

For Indian cricket fans, the series represents a chance to see their team perform in sub‑continental conditions against a historic opponent. A competitive West Indies side can raise the standard of play, making the games more attractive to broadcasters and sponsors. In the Caribbean, the financial argument put forward by the WICB chief highlights a growing concern that revenue from high‑profile series is not reaching smaller cricketing nations in proportion to the costs they incur. If the ICC does not address these disparities, boards like the WICB may struggle to fund grassroots programmes, player development and infrastructure.

The Hyderabad win, credited to slower deliveries, also underscores the tactical evolution within T20 cricket. Teams that can adapt their bowling strategies quickly gain a competitive edge, which can influence coaching methods across the sub‑continent. For Indian viewers, the match offers insight into how opposition teams can exploit specific weaknesses, prompting discussions about batting technique and preparation.

Finally, the potential changes to the West Indies playing XI for the fifth T20 could affect betting markets, viewership numbers, and the overall narrative of the series. A refreshed lineup might revive interest among Indian audiences, who closely follow the performance of overseas teams.

Overall, the financial grievances, tactical revelations, and selection decisions intertwine to shape both the immediate sporting contest and the longer‑term health of cricket in the region.

Background

The West Indies cricket team, representing a collection of Caribbean nations, has historically been a powerhouse in the sport, especially during the 1970s and 1980s. In recent years, however, the team has faced challenges both on and off the field, including inconsistent performances and financial constraints. The ICC distributes revenue from global tournaments and bilateral series based on a formula that favours larger markets, leaving smaller boards to rely heavily on match‑day income and sponsorship.

India, as the world\'s largest cricket market, generates substantial broadcast and commercial revenue. When the Indian team tours the Caribbean or hosts the West Indies, the financial dynamics shift dramatically. The WICB has previously expressed concerns that the revenue share from such high‑profile series does not cover the costs of travel, accommodation, and player contracts.

The current T20 series is part of the ICC\'s Future Tours Programme, designed to ensure regular competition among member nations. The series began with India taking an early lead, but the West Indies managed to turn the tide in Hyderabad, thanks in part to strategic bowling changes. This victory marked the first win for the West Indies on Indian soil in a T20 match in over a decade, adding a layer of significance to the ongoing discussions about competitiveness and financial sustainability.

What happens next

The fifth T20 is scheduled to take place in Bengaluru later this week. The West Indies selectors are expected to announce a revised XI that may include an additional seam bowler and a versatile all‑rounder to bolster the middle order. The outcome of the match will likely influence the board\'s negotiations with the ICC regarding revenue sharing, as a series win could strengthen their bargaining position.

Meanwhile, the ICC is under pressure from several associate and full‑member boards to review its distribution model. Stakeholders anticipate that the upcoming ICC council meeting, slated for early next year, will address these concerns, potentially leading to a more balanced financial framework for future tours.
