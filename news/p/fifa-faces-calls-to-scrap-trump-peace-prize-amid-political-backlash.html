FIFA faces calls to scrap Trump peace prize amid political backlash

FIFA’s newly created Peace Prize, first awarded to former US President Donald Trump, has sparked a political firestorm. Senior FIFA figures are urging the governing body to either scrap the award or strip it of political connotations.

Latest developments

Senior officials within FIFA have reportedly urged President Gianni Infantino to either scrap the Peace Prize or remove any political overtones after the award was presented to former US President Donald Trump. The criticism intensified following a public outcry in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, where many fans and commentators questioned the suitability of a political figure receiving a football‑related honour.

In response, FIFA announced the formation of an independent panel to examine the circumstances surrounding the inaugural award. The panel, composed of former players, administrators and legal experts, is tasked with reviewing the award’s criteria, selection process and the decision to name Trump as the first recipient. Its mandate includes recommending whether the prize should continue, be rebranded or be discontinued altogether.

The United States media reported that the review will be completed before the end of the calendar year, with a final report to be presented to the FIFA Council. Infantino is expected to address the council’s members in a special session, where he will outline any proposed reforms.

Critics argue that the award, introduced only last year, was never meant to become a platform for political endorsement. They point to FIFA’s statutes, which stress the sport’s role in promoting peace and unity, as evidence that the prize should remain strictly apolitical. Supporters of the prize, however, claim that recognising high‑profile leaders can raise the profile of football’s peace‑building initiatives.

The controversy has also drawn attention from sponsors and broadcasters, who are monitoring the situation closely. Some commercial partners have issued statements emphasizing their commitment to a neutral sport, while others await the panel’s findings before making any public comment.

Why it matters

For football fans in Great Britain, the dispute touches on long‑standing concerns about the politicisation of sport. Many supporters view FIFA’s global influence as a barometer for how football interacts with broader societal issues. The perception that a governing body could be used to legitimise a polarising political figure threatens to erode trust among a constituency that values the game’s traditional separation from partisan politics.

The issue also has financial implications. Television rights holders and advertisers in the UK have previously warned that association with controversial decisions could affect viewership and brand reputation. A decision to retain the prize in its current form may prompt sponsors to reassess their contracts, potentially impacting revenue streams that fund grassroots programmes and women's football development.

Beyond economics, the debate reflects a wider cultural conversation about the role of sport in diplomacy. If FIFA chooses to distance itself from the Trump award, it may set a precedent for stricter governance of future honours, reinforcing the idea that football should serve as a neutral platform for peace rather than a vehicle for political endorsement.

Background

The FIFA Peace Prize was introduced in early 2025 as part of the organisation’s commitment to using football as a tool for conflict resolution and social cohesion. The award was intended to recognise individuals or organisations that had made a significant contribution to peace through the sport’s global reach. Its inaugural recipient, Donald Trump, was selected after a closed‑door nomination process that cited his involvement in several diplomatic initiatives during his presidency.

The decision to honour Trump immediately attracted scrutiny. Critics argued that his tenure was marked by polarising policies and that his approach to international relations did not align with the prize’s stated objectives. Supporters within FIFA’s leadership, however, pointed to specific diplomatic engagements, such as the historic summit with North Korea, as evidence of a peace‑building impact.

Since its launch, the Peace Prize has not been awarded to any other individual or organisation. The lack of a clear, transparent selection framework has been highlighted as a weakness, prompting calls for a review of the prize’s governance before any further recipients are named.

What happens next

The independent panel will deliver its findings to the FIFA Council by late December 2026. Possible outcomes include the complete abolition of the Peace Prize, a redefinition of its criteria to exclude political figures, or the introduction of a new, apolitical award structure. Infantino is expected to announce any changes at the next FIFA Congress in early 2027.

Stakeholders, including national associations and commercial partners, have indicated they will monitor the process closely. A decision that aligns the prize with the sport’s non‑political ethos is likely to restore confidence among fans and sponsors, while a decision to retain the award in its current form could fuel further debate and potential boycotts.
