Russia Sends Deputy Prime Minister to Kabul to Deepen Ties with Taliban

Moscow dispatches Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak to Kabul for talks on trade and security with the Taliban. The visit signals a shift in regional dynamics that could affect Pakistan's interests.

Latest developments

Moscow announced that Deputy Prime Minister for Economic Development, Alexander Novak, will travel to Kabul on Tuesday for a series of meetings aimed at expanding economic cooperation with the Taliban‑run administration. The visit follows a recent encounter in Kabul between the Taliban’s interior minister, Sirajuddin Haqqani, and Russia’s special envoy for Afghanistan, Dmitry Zhirnov, where both sides expressed a willingness to coordinate on security and trade issues. In a separate statement, Russia’s ambassador to the United Nations Security Council, Dmitry Polyanski, urged the international community to pursue constructive cooperation with the Taliban, arguing that engagement is essential for regional stability. Analysts in Islamabad note that the timing of the Russian outreach coincides with heightened Pakistani concerns over cross‑border militancy and the need for alternative economic partners as Western aid to Afghanistan remains limited. The delegation is expected to discuss possibilities for Russian investment in Afghan mining projects, the reopening of trade routes linking Central Asia with the Indian Ocean, and joint efforts to combat drug trafficking that affects both nations. During the visit, Novak is also slated to meet Afghan officials responsible for the Lapis corridor, a historic trade route that could be revitalised to move goods from Central Asia to the Arabian Sea, a project that Russia has indicated interest in financing.

Why it matters

For Pakistan, Russia’s growing presence in Kabul could reshape the balance of influence in a neighbourhood where Islamabad has traditionally played a leading diplomatic role. A deeper economic link between Moscow and the Taliban may provide new markets for Pakistani exporters and reduce the flow of illicit goods that cross the porous border, a concern for Pakistani security agencies. At the same time, Islamabad watches closely to ensure that any Russian‑Taliban cooperation does not sideline Pakistani interests, especially in the context of the China‑Pakistan Economic Corridor and ongoing talks about water sharing.

Background

Since the U.S. withdrawal in August 2021, the Taliban have sought international recognition and economic assistance, while most Western capitals have kept diplomatic ties at a minimal level. Russia, which maintains a strategic interest in Central Asia, has gradually increased its diplomatic outreach, opening a liaison office in Kabul in 2022 and engaging with Taliban officials on security and narcotics issues. Pakistan, sharing a long border and cultural ties with Afghanistan, has been a key conduit for humanitarian aid and has hosted several rounds of talks between the Taliban and neighboring states, positioning itself as a regional stabiliser.

What happens next

The upcoming talks are likely to produce a memorandum of understanding on trade and security cooperation, though concrete projects will depend on the Taliban’s ability to guarantee safe passage for Russian personnel and goods. Both Moscow and Islamabad are expected to monitor the outcomes closely, with Pakistani officials preparing to engage with Russian diplomats to align any new initiatives with Pakistan’s own security and economic priorities.
