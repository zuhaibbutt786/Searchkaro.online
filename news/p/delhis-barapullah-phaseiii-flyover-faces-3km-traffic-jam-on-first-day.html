Delhi’s Barapullah Phase‑III Flyover Faces 3‑km Traffic Jam on First Day

The newly opened Barapullah Phase‑III flyover in Delhi quickly became a bottleneck, with a 3‑km stretch of traffic snarling during the morning peak. Officials are assessing the cause as commuters report delays of up to an hour.

Latest developments

The Barapullah Phase‑III flyover, inaugurated on Monday, began operating on Tuesday but soon encountered severe congestion. Traffic monitoring data showed a continuous line of vehicles extending roughly 3.5 kilometres from the flyover's entry point, effectively turning the morning rush hour into an afternoon ordeal. Seven lanes that were intended to streamline flow were merged into three, creating a choke point that forced drivers onto the adjoining surface roads.

Commuters posted real‑time updates on social media, noting that travel times that normally take 15 minutes were stretching to 45 minutes or more. The Delhi Traffic Police confirmed that the jam persisted well beyond the typical peak window, with some sections remaining gridlocked for over an hour. Emergency services reported that the slowdown had also impacted the response time for ambulances and fire units operating in the vicinity.

City officials attributed the bottleneck to a combination of high vehicle volume and incomplete integration of entry and exit ramps. Engineers are reviewing the lane‑allocation plan, which was designed to accommodate an estimated 150,000 vehicles per day. Preliminary assessments suggest that the merging of seven lanes into three may have been overly optimistic given the actual traffic load on the first day.

The situation prompted the Municipal Corporation of Delhi to issue an advisory urging commuters to use alternative routes, such as the nearby Mahatma Gandhi Road and the Ring Road, until the flyover's traffic pattern stabilises. Public transport agencies also increased the frequency of buses on parallel corridors to alleviate pressure on private vehicles.

Why it matters

Delhi’s road network is already strained by a growing number of private vehicles, and any disruption on a major artery can ripple across the city. The Barapullah Phase‑III flyover was promoted as a solution to chronic congestion on the Barapullah corridor, which links the northern and eastern parts of the capital. Its early failure raises questions about the planning and execution of large‑scale infrastructure projects in densely populated urban areas.

For daily commuters, the jam translates into lost work hours, higher fuel consumption, and increased stress. Small businesses that rely on timely deliveries also face setbacks, potentially affecting local economies. Moreover, prolonged traffic queues contribute to higher emissions, undermining Delhi’s ongoing efforts to improve air quality.

The incident also highlights the challenge of synchronising road design with real‑world driver behaviour. While engineers may model traffic flow under ideal conditions, actual patterns can differ dramatically when drivers encounter unfamiliar lane configurations or inadequate signage. Understanding these gaps is essential for future projects that aim to ease mobility rather than exacerbate it.

Background

The Barapullah Phase‑III flyover is part of a broader initiative to upgrade Delhi’s transport infrastructure ahead of the 2024 national elections and the upcoming Commonwealth Games. The project, costing approximately INR 1,200 crore, was intended to provide a seamless link between the Barapullah bridge and the Delhi‑Meerut Expressway, reducing travel time between the city centre and the eastern suburbs.

Construction began in 2019, with the flyover designed to span 2.2 kilometres and feature seven lanes—four for north‑bound traffic and three for south‑bound. The structure includes a mix of elevated sections and at‑grade intersections, intended to distribute traffic evenly across the network. Prior to opening, officials conducted trial runs and reported smooth operation, though these tests were limited to controlled conditions.

Delhi’s traffic management authority has historically faced criticism for underestimating demand on new roads. Similar issues were observed after the inauguration of the Delhi‑Gurgaon Expressway in 2018, where initial optimism gave way to congestion complaints within weeks. These precedents underscore the importance of post‑opening monitoring and rapid response mechanisms.

What happens next

The municipal engineering department has scheduled an on‑site assessment for the coming week to recalibrate lane usage and improve ramp connectivity. Traffic police are expected to deploy additional personnel to manage flow during peak periods, while the public transport department will continue to promote alternative routes and increase bus frequencies. A detailed report on the flyover’s performance is slated for release within the next month.
