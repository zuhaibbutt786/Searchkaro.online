Latest developments

The Senate has become the latest battleground for the long‑debated issue of daylight saving time, as a bipartisan group of lawmakers has introduced a bill to end the practice. The proposal, which would keep clocks on standard time year‑round, has faced strong opposition from several Republican senators, most notably Arkansas's Tom Cotton. Cotton has argued that the change would disrupt the economy and harm the energy sector, and he has vowed to use his seniority to block the measure. In a dramatic turn, former President Donald Trump joined the fray by urging his supporters to contact Cotton directly. On October 3, the New York Times reported that Trump posted Cotton’s phone number on his social media accounts, encouraging followers to call and express their concerns about the bill. The move drew criticism from Cotton’s colleagues, who called the action an inappropriate intrusion into Senate business. The Senate’s rules on filibuster and cloture have made it difficult for the daylight saving bill to advance. While the House passed a similar measure in 2025, the Senate has yet to hold a vote. Cotton’s opposition is seen as a key obstacle, as he controls the committee that would review the proposal and can use his influence to stall proceedings. Political analysts note that the clash reflects broader tensions over the role of the federal government in regulating time zones. Some argue that a uniform standard would simplify schedules across the country, while others fear that the change could negatively affect businesses that rely on seasonal adjustments. Cotton’s stance is rooted in a long‑standing tradition of preserving the status quo in matters of timekeeping.

Why it matters

For Americans, the debate over daylight saving time has practical implications. The practice shifts the sun’s position by an hour, affecting sleep patterns, energy consumption, and daily routines. A permanent standard‑time policy could reduce the need for artificial lighting in the evening, potentially lowering electricity use, but it may also lead to darker mornings, especially in northern states. The economic impact is a major concern for businesses that operate across time zones. Retailers, airlines, and financial markets rely on predictable schedules, and a sudden change could disrupt operations. Cotton argues that the current system, despite its quirks, provides a flexible framework that accommodates the diverse needs of different regions. Public opinion on daylight saving time is divided, with polls indicating that a majority of voters favor keeping the current system. The debate also intersects with health studies that link the biannual clock changes to increased heart attacks and workplace accidents. As such, the Senate’s decision will have lasting effects on public health and economic stability.

Background

Daylight saving time was first introduced in the United States during World War I as a way to conserve fuel. It was later adopted nationwide in 1966 under the Uniform Time Act, which allowed states to opt out but required a consistent schedule across the country. Over the decades, the practice has been criticized for its health risks and minimal energy savings. In recent years, several states have passed legislation to remain on permanent standard time, but federal law still mandates the biannual clock changes. The current bill, known as the 'Sunshine Standard Time Act,' seeks to eliminate the practice entirely. It has bipartisan support in the House but faces a filibuster in the Senate, where the Senate Majority Leader has signaled a cautious approach to time‑related reforms.

What happens next

The next step for the bill is a Senate committee hearing, where Cotton will likely testify against the measure. If the committee approves a motion to advance, the full Senate will need to invoke cloture to overcome the filibuster, a process that requires a 60‑vote threshold. Meanwhile, Trump’s public pressure may influence public opinion and put additional scrutiny on Cotton’s position. However, without a change in Senate rules or a shift in the majority’s stance, the bill remains unlikely to pass in its current form.
