India-Bangladesh Ganges Water Treaty Nears Expiry, Bihar Demands Fresh Share

The 30‑year Ganges water sharing accord between India and Bangladesh is set to lapse, sparking diplomatic overtures from Dhaka and fresh demands from Bihar. Both sides face pressure to secure water for agriculture and flood control.

Latest developments

Bangladesh’s foreign ministry has formally urged New Delhi to start negotiations on a new Ganges water sharing agreement as the current treaty approaches its expiry date later this year. The appeal, reported by Dawn, underscores Dhaka’s concern that a lapse could disrupt the flow of water to the northern districts that rely on the river for irrigation and drinking supplies. In New Delhi, the Ministry of Water Resources has not yet issued a detailed response, but senior officials are said to be reviewing the implications for downstream states.

In the Indian state of Bihar, officials have intensified their lobbying for a larger water allocation under any successor treaty. According to Indian Masterminds, Bihar is seeking an additional 10,000 cusecs of water from the Ganges, a figure that would significantly boost the state’s agricultural output during the critical monsoon season. The state government also called for a national silt management policy to address the heavy sediment load that reduces water conveyance efficiency.

Scroll.in highlighted that Bihar’s demand is driven by recurring water shortages and flood‑related damage that have plagued the region for years. State leaders argue that without a guaranteed share, the livelihoods of millions of farmers remain vulnerable. The pressure from Bihar adds a domestic dimension to the bilateral talks, as India must balance its own internal water needs with the obligations to Bangladesh.

Both Bangladesh and Bihar have scheduled high‑level meetings with the central government in the coming weeks. While Bangladesh seeks a cooperative framework that ensures equitable distribution, Bihar pushes for a more assertive stance that could reshape the allocation formula. The outcome of these discussions will shape water security for a densely populated stretch of South Asia.

Why it matters

For Pakistan, the Ganges treaty’s expiration carries indirect but significant implications. The Ganges and its tributaries feed into the Indus basin through cross‑border groundwater and seasonal flows. Any reduction in water availability downstream of India could alter the hydrological balance, affecting the Indus‑fed agricultural zones in Punjab and Sindh. Moreover, heightened competition for water in the Ganges basin may prompt Pakistan to reassess its own water‑sharing agreements with India, especially under the Indus Waters Treaty.

Domestically, the issue resonates with Pakistani citizens who are already facing water scarcity in many urban and rural areas. Media outlets in Pakistan have begun covering the negotiations, framing them as a test of regional cooperation on shared resources. A stable agreement could set a precedent for collaborative water management, while a breakdown might exacerbate tensions over trans‑border rivers, a historically sensitive topic in South‑Asian geopolitics.

Economically, the Ganges supports a vast agricultural economy in Bihar and northern Bangladesh. Disruptions could lead to lower crop yields, higher food prices, and increased migration, all of which could have spill‑over effects on Pakistan’s trade and labor markets. Hence, the treaty’s fate is closely watched by policymakers and analysts across the subcontinent.

Background

The original Ganges water sharing treaty, signed in 1996, allocated a fixed volume of water to Bangladesh based on seasonal flow measurements. The agreement, which was set for a 30‑year term, has been credited with reducing water‑related disputes between the two countries and providing a predictable supply for Bangladesh’s northern districts. Over the years, however, climate change, upstream dam projects, and increased water demand have strained the original allocation.

India’s internal water politics have also evolved. States such as Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, and West Bengal have grown in population and agricultural intensity, leading to higher water consumption. In recent years, Bihar has repeatedly raised concerns that the current share is insufficient for its irrigation needs, especially during years of low monsoon rainfall. The state’s call for a national silt management policy reflects broader challenges of sedimentation that diminish river capacity and exacerbate flood risks.

Bangladesh, meanwhile, has pursued a policy of diplomatic engagement to protect its water security. The country’s reliance on the Ganges for both agriculture and drinking water makes any reduction in flow a matter of national importance. Past negotiations have produced minor adjustments, but the looming expiry of the treaty has renewed calls for a comprehensive review that could incorporate climate resilience and joint river‑basin management mechanisms.

What happens next

The Indian government is expected to convene a joint committee with Bangladeshi officials within the next two months to outline the framework for a new treaty. Simultaneously, Bihar will likely submit a formal memorandum to the central water ministry demanding the 10,000 cusecs allocation and a clear silt‑control strategy. The success of these parallel tracks will depend on how India balances its domestic pressures with its diplomatic commitments.

If a new agreement is reached before the current treaty lapses, it could ensure uninterrupted water flow and set a collaborative tone for future basin‑wide initiatives. Failure to agree, however, may lead to unilateral water management actions, heightened regional tension, and increased pressure on Pakistan’s own water security calculations.
