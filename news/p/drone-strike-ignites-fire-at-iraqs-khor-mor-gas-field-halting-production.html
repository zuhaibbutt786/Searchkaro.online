Drone strike ignites fire at Iraq's Khor Mor gas field, halting production

A drone laden with explosives hit Iraq's largest onshore gas field, sparking a massive fire and forcing a production shutdown. The incident comes as US‑Iran tensions flare, prompting worries about regional energy stability.

Latest developments

On 8 November 2026, a drone loaded with explosives struck the Khor Mor gas field in northern Iraq, igniting a large fire that quickly engulfed the processing facilities. The field, operated by a joint venture that includes the state‑owned Iraq National Oil Company, confirmed that production had been suspended pending a safety assessment. Satellite imagery released by independent observers showed a plume of smoke rising from the site, and local emergency crews were seen battling the blaze for several hours. No immediate casualties were reported, but the damage to infrastructure is expected to be extensive. The attack occurred amid heightened tensions following a series of strikes in the Persian Gulf, where Iran accused the United States of conducting missile and drone operations against its facilities on Qeshm Island. Iranian state media linked the northern Iraq incident to the same campaign, suggesting that the same type of unmanned aircraft was used. While the United States has not confirmed involvement, officials in Washington have warned Tehran that further escalation could jeopardise regional stability and disrupt key oil and gas corridors that pass through Iraq and neighboring countries. Khor Mor is Iraq’s largest onshore gas field, accounting for roughly 30 percent of the country’s total gas output. The sudden shutdown therefore raises concerns about domestic power generation, as natural gas supplies a significant share of electricity production in Baghdad and other major cities. Analysts note that reduced output could force the government to increase imports of liquefied natural gas (LNG) or to rely more heavily on diesel generators, both of which would raise energy costs for consumers. The incident also adds pressure on Iraq’s already fragile energy infrastructure, which has struggled to meet growing demand since the 2003 conflict. Iraqi authorities have launched an investigation to identify the drone’s origin and to assess the extent of the damage. The Ministry of Oil announced that security forces would increase patrols around critical energy sites and that technical teams would begin repairs as soon as the fire is fully extinguished. In a brief statement, the Iraqi Prime Minister’s office called the attack “an act of aggression that threatens national security” and urged neighboring states to cooperate in preventing further incidents.

Why it matters

For Pakistan, the disruption of Iraq’s gas supply could have indirect effects on regional energy markets. Both countries import a portion of their oil and gas through the Persian Gulf, and any instability in Iraq’s production capacity may tighten global LNG prices, which in turn influence the cost of electricity and transportation fuel in Karachi and Lahore. Traders in Islamabad have already noted a slight uptick in spot prices for Middle Eastern gas contracts following the attack, prompting concerns among policymakers about potential inflationary pressure. Security analysts also warn that the incident could embolden militant groups operating along Iraq’s northern borders, some of which have established links with factions in Pakistan’s tribal areas. An escalation of drone warfare may lead to tighter border controls and affect the flow of goods through the overland routes that connect the two nations via Iran, potentially disrupting trade in textiles, agricultural products, and construction materials that are vital to Pakistan’s export economy.

Background

Iraq’s oil and gas sector has been a cornerstone of its post‑war reconstruction, with the Khor Mor field discovered in 1972 and brought into production in the early 2000s. The field’s reserves are estimated at over 10 trillion cubic feet of natural gas, making it a strategic asset for meeting domestic energy needs and for export contracts with neighboring countries, including Turkey and Jordan. The region has seen a rise in the use of armed drones since the early 2020s, with several state and non‑state actors employing them to strike high‑value infrastructure. Iran’s conflict with Israel and the United States has spilled over into neighboring territories, and Iraq’s relatively porous northern border has become a conduit for such unmanned systems. Previous incidents, including attacks on oil pipelines in 2024, have highlighted the vulnerability of Iraq’s energy network to aerial threats.

What happens next

International observers expect a joint Iraqi‑U.S. technical team to assess the damage within the next few days, while diplomatic channels in Tehran and Washington will likely exchange accusations and demands for accountability. The Iraqi government has indicated that production could resume once safety checks are completed, but analysts caution that full restoration may take weeks, depending on the extent of equipment loss and the availability of replacement parts.
