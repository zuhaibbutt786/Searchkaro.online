UN Mission in Western Sahara Faces Uncertain Future After Draft Stalls

Latest developments

The head of the United Nations Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara (MINURSO) convened an emergency session with senior officials of the mission on Monday, seeking a rapid response to the growing diplomatic impasse. The meeting, described as "urgent" by the mission's spokesperson, focused on the immediate need to reassess the operational timeline and to coordinate with both the Moroccan and Algerian authorities.

Algeria issued a strong statement on Tuesday rejecting the United Nations’ decision not to renew MINURSO’s mandate. The Algerian foreign ministry called the move "a clear bias toward Morocco" and warned that any withdrawal could destabilise the fragile cease‑fire that has held since 1991. The statement was echoed in regional media, highlighting the deep mistrust between Algiers and Rabat over the status of the disputed territory.

Parallel diplomatic efforts in New York faltered when two separate draft resolutions – one sponsored by the United States and the other by Russia – failed to secure the required votes in the Security Council. The U.S. draft sought to extend the mission while encouraging a negotiated political solution, whereas the Russian text emphasized a more limited role for the UN and called for a renewed referendum. Neither text achieved consensus, leaving the Council without a clear path forward.

The failure of the resolutions has left MINURSO without a renewed mandate, raising the prospect that the mission could wind down its operations by the end of the year. Analysts note that the mission’s primary tasks – monitoring the cease‑fire and preparing for a self‑determination referendum – have been stalled for years, and the lack of a political breakthrough makes the continuation of a costly peacekeeping presence increasingly difficult.

Why it matters

For readers in Saudi Arabia, the uncertainty surrounding MINURSO is relevant on several fronts. The Kingdom has long pursued a stable North African neighbourhood to protect its energy trade routes and to maintain a predictable security environment. Any escalation in the Western Sahara dispute could affect oil and gas shipments that pass through the Mediterranean and the Atlantic, potentially influencing global prices that Saudi markets monitor closely.

A sudden vacuum in the cease‑fire monitoring could also trigger a rise in cross‑border smuggling and irregular migration, issues that Saudi authorities have highlighted in recent security forums. The Kingdom’s cooperation with both Morocco and Algeria on counter‑terrorism initiatives means that a deterioration in their bilateral relations could complicate joint efforts against extremist networks operating in the Sahel and Maghreb regions.

Background

Western Sahara, a former Spanish colony, was claimed by both Morocco and the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic (SADR) after Spain withdrew in 1975. A brief war between Morocco, Mauritania and the Polisario Front – the liberation movement representing the SADR – ended with a cease‑fire in 1991, brokered by the United Nations. That same year, the UN established MINURSO to monitor the cease‑fire and to organise a referendum on self‑determination, a promise that has never been fulfilled.

The referendum has been stalled for decades due to disagreements over voter eligibility and the political status of the territory. Morocco has since proposed an autonomy plan that would keep Western Sahara under its sovereignty while granting limited self‑governance. Algeria, which hosts Sahrawi refugees and supports the Polisario, rejects the autonomy proposal and continues to push for a full independence referendum.

MINURSO’s presence has been a stabilising factor, but its mandate has been repeatedly extended without progress toward a final settlement. The mission’s budget and personnel levels have been a point of contention, especially as donor fatigue grows and the international community focuses on other crises.

What happens next

The United Nations Security Council is expected to convene a special session within the next two weeks to discuss the future of MINURSO. Options on the table include a short‑term extension of the mandate, a reconfiguration of the mission’s tasks, or a complete withdrawal. Member states are likely to weigh the political costs of each scenario against the financial implications of maintaining a largely inert peacekeeping force.

Regional actors, including the African Union, may also seek to mediate a new framework for dialogue between Morocco and the Polisario. Any breakthrough will require confidence‑building measures, such as the release of detained activists and the opening of humanitarian corridors for Sahrawi refugees. The outcome will shape the security calculus for neighboring countries and could influence Saudi diplomatic initiatives in the broader Maghreb.
