Pakistan's central bank lifts policy rate to 12% amid rising inflation

The State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) has increased its policy rate by half a percentage point, taking the benchmark to 12%. The move is intended to tame inflation that has surged in recent months.

Latest developments

The State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) announced on Tuesday that it will raise its policy rate by 50 basis points, moving the benchmark from 11.5% to 12%. The decision, reported by Dawn, marks the central bank’s most aggressive tightening move since the start of the fiscal year. SBP Governor Jameel Ahmad cited persistent price pressures and a weakening rupee as primary reasons for the hike. The increase is part of a broader effort to restore price stability and protect the purchasing power of households. In its press release, the bank emphasized that the adjustment is a 'necessary step' to anchor inflation expectations amid a volatile external environment. Analysts expect the move to put additional pressure on borrowing costs for businesses and consumers alike, while also signaling the bank’s commitment to meeting its inflation target of 6-10%. Pakistan’s consumer price index has been climbing steadily, reaching 21.5% year‑on‑year in August, well above the SBP’s medium‑term target band. The latest figures released by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics show food prices alone surged by more than 30% over the same period, intensifying the burden on low‑income families. Prior to this decision, the central bank had already lifted rates twice this year, first by 25 basis points in March and another 25 in June, bringing the policy rate to 11.5%. The cumulative tightening reflects the bank’s response to a widening current‑account deficit and a depreciating rupee that has lost roughly 15% against the dollar since the start of the fiscal year. The steep rise in food inflation has prompted the government to consider targeted subsidies, but the central bank maintains that monetary tightening remains the primary tool to bring overall price growth under control. Financial markets reacted swiftly to the announcement. The Karachi Stock Exchange’s KSE‑100 index slipped 1.2% in early trading, while the Pakistani rupee depreciated marginally against the US dollar, reflecting investors’ concerns over higher financing costs. Business Recorder’s coverage highlighted that the rate hike could tighten credit conditions, potentially slowing growth in sectors such as construction and manufacturing that are already vulnerable to rising input costs. However, some analysts noted that a credible monetary stance may eventually restore confidence among foreign investors, which could help stabilise the balance of payments over the medium term. The central bank’s decision also aligns with recent moves by regional peers, including the Reserve Bank of India, which has similarly raised rates to combat inflationary pressures. International observers, including Mettis Global, echoed the local media’s assessment, describing the 0.5 percentage‑point increase as a necessary calibration in the face of persistent price pressures. The agency pointed out that Pakistan’s inflation trajectory remains among the highest in South Asia, and that continued policy tightening may be required if the current trend does not reverse. At the same time, the SBP warned that further hikes would be data‑dependent, leaving room for future adjustments should inflation show signs of easing. The central bank’s forward guidance underscores its commitment to the 6‑10% inflation target while balancing the need to support economic activity and avoid a sharp slowdown.

Why it matters

The rate hike directly affects Pakistani households by increasing the cost of borrowing for mortgages, auto loans and personal credit. Higher interest rates translate into larger monthly repayments, squeezing disposable income at a time when food prices are soaring. For many families already coping with limited wage growth, the added financial pressure could push more households into poverty or force them to cut back on essential consumption, thereby feeding back into overall inflation dynamics. For businesses, especially small and medium‑sized enterprises, the higher policy rate raises the benchmark for commercial loans, making expansion financing more expensive. Companies reliant on imported inputs may also face higher costs as the rupee continues to weaken, potentially eroding profit margins. Investors watching Pakistan’s sovereign debt may demand higher yields to compensate for perceived risk, which could increase the government’s borrowing costs and limit fiscal space for development projects.

Background

The State Bank of Pakistan, established in 1948, is the country’s central monetary authority responsible for formulating and implementing monetary policy, managing foreign exchange reserves, and ensuring financial stability. Its primary mandate, as stipulated by the State Bank of Pakistan Act, is to maintain price stability while supporting economic growth. The bank’s policy rate, also known as the benchmark interest rate, serves as a reference for commercial banks’ lending and deposit rates across the economy. Pakistan has struggled with high inflation for much of the past decade, driven by a combination of supply‑side constraints, fiscal deficits, and external shocks such as volatile oil prices. In 2022, inflation peaked above 30%, prompting the SBP to embark on a series of aggressive rate hikes. Over the last twelve months, the central bank has raised the policy rate by a total of 75 basis points before today’s additional 50‑basis‑point increase, reflecting a gradual but steady tightening cycle. External pressures, including a widening current‑account deficit and a depreciating rupee, have limited the central bank’s policy space. Moreover, global monetary tightening by major economies has increased the cost of external financing for emerging markets, adding to Pakistan’s vulnerability. These factors have compelled the SBP to balance the need for inflation control with the risk of stifling growth.
