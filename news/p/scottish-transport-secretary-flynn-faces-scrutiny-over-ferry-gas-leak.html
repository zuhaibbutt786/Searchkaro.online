Scottish Transport Secretary Flynn faces scrutiny over ferry gas leak

Stephen Flynn, Scotland's transport secretary, was found to have been emailed about a ferry gas leak before publicly denying any prior knowledge. The revelation has led to a heated parliamentary committee hearing.

Latest developments

The story emerged after a BBC report that Stephen Flynn, Scotland's transport secretary, received an email concerning a gas leak on a ferry operating between mainland Scotland and the islands. The email, dated weeks before the incident became public, indicated that officials were aware of a potential safety issue but the details were not disclosed to the public. Flynn later denied any prior knowledge when questioned by the Scottish Parliament's transport committee.

The Times obtained a separate briefing document that was sent to Flynn's office ahead of his appearance before the committee. The briefing outlined the timeline of the leak, the vessels involved, and the steps taken by the Marine Accident Investigation Branch. It also noted that the leak was contained without injuries, but the lack of immediate public notification raised concerns about transparency.

On Tuesday, Flynn appeared before the committee and was pressed on why he had not mentioned the email or the briefing in his initial statements. He maintained that the information had been part of routine internal communications and that no formal decision had been made to alert the public until the situation was fully assessed.

First Minister Humza Yousaf, accompanied by Deputy First Minister John Swinney, responded to the committee's questioning with a measured tone. Swinney, described by Holyrood as “solemn,” emphasized the government's commitment to safety and promised a full review of communication protocols. The FM’s serious demeanor was noted as an attempt to restore confidence after the perceived mishandling.

Opposition MSPs criticised the government's approach, arguing that the delay in informing passengers and local communities could have eroded trust in transport services. They called for an independent inquiry into the decision‑making process and for clearer guidelines on when to disclose emerging safety risks.

Why it matters

The ferry route is a lifeline for island communities, carrying commuters, tourists, and essential supplies. Any interruption or safety incident can have disproportionate economic and social effects, making timely communication crucial.

The episode also highlights broader concerns about how the Scottish Government handles emerging hazards in transport infrastructure. Public confidence in safety oversight can influence policy support and future investment in ferry services.

Additionally, the scrutiny of Flynn's handling may affect the political standing of the ruling SNP, especially as the party prepares for upcoming local elections. Demonstrating accountability could mitigate potential backlash.

Background

The gas leak was first detected on the MV Caledonian Isles, a vessel that serves the Oban‑Islay route. Sensors indicated a rise in methane levels in the engine room, prompting an emergency shutdown and a temporary suspension of service while engineers repaired the system.

Scotland's ferry network is managed by Caledonian MacBrayne (CalMac), a state‑owned operator overseen by the transport secretary. Past incidents, such as the 2022 grounding of the MV Loch Seaforth, have already put pressure on the department to improve safety standards.

The Scottish Parliament's transport committee regularly reviews incidents to ensure lessons are learned. The current investigation follows a pattern of increased parliamentary scrutiny of transport safety after several high‑profile failures across the UK.

What happens next

The committee has scheduled a follow‑up session in two weeks to examine the full briefing and to hear from the Marine Accident Investigation Branch. Flynn has pledged to cooperate fully and to release a detailed report to the public.

Opposition parties have urged the First Minister to commission an independent inquiry, a move that could lead to recommendations on communication protocols and possibly legislative changes to enforce earlier public disclosure of safety risks.
