Dry Spell Hits Central India as Low Pressure Spurs Cyclone Threat

Latest developments

The Indian Meteorological Department (IMD) reported that much of central India, including the capital region of Amaravati, is under a pronounced dry spell today. Temperatures have risen above 38°C in several districts, while relative humidity has dipped to single‑digit levels, prompting concerns among local farmers about soil moisture depletion. The situation mirrors a similar pattern noted by Prajasakti, which highlighted the abrupt shift from the usual pre‑monsoon showers to an extended period of aridity. Residents have been advised to conserve water and limit outdoor activities during peak heat hours.

At the same time, a low‑pressure system has deepened over the Bay of Bengal, prompting the IMD to issue a rain alert for several coastal states. The system is expected to bring scattered showers to parts of Telangana and Andhra Pradesh, with the possibility of localized thunderstorms. Asianet News Telugu reported that the low pressure is also associated with a cold wave moving inland, creating a contrast between the scorching interior and cooler breezes along the coast. The agency warned that sudden temperature drops could affect vulnerable populations, especially the elderly and those with respiratory conditions.

Further compounding the weather picture, the IMD has identified a developing cyclonic disturbance that could affect the south coast of Andhra Pradesh. Sakshi highlighted that the system, still classified as a deep depression, carries the potential to intensify into a cyclonic storm within the next 48 hours. Coastal districts such as Nellore and Prakasam are being placed under heightened vigilance, with authorities preparing evacuation plans and pre‑positioning relief supplies. The threat has raised alarms among fishermen, who have been urged to remain in safe harbors until conditions improve.

In response to the converging weather phenomena, the IMD issued a comprehensive advisory that combines heat‑wave precautions for the interior, rain and cold‑wave alerts for the eastern corridor, and cyclone preparedness measures for the southern coastline. State disaster management agencies have activated early warning systems, and local administrations are coordinating with health departments to set up cooling centers and distribution points for drinking water. The multi‑faceted approach reflects the department’s effort to mitigate the disparate impacts of extreme heat, sudden rainfall, and possible cyclonic activity across a broad swath of the country.

Why it matters

The dry spell in central India threatens agricultural output at a critical stage of the cropping cycle. Wheat and pulses, which rely on residual soil moisture before the monsoon onset, face reduced yields if irrigation cannot compensate for the lack of rain. Water scarcity also strains municipal supplies, prompting authorities to enforce rationing measures that affect daily life for millions of residents. Meanwhile, the low‑pressure system’s rain alerts raise the risk of flash floods in low‑lying urban areas, where inadequate drainage can quickly turn brief showers into hazardous waterlogging, disrupting traffic and commerce.

For the coastal regions of Andhra Pradesh, the looming cyclone poses a dual danger: heavy rainfall that can trigger landslides in the Eastern Ghats and storm surges that threaten coastal settlements. The economic impact could be significant, as fisheries, tourism, and port operations are vulnerable to prolonged disruptions. Early warnings aim to reduce loss of life, but the potential damage to infrastructure and livelihoods underscores the importance of coordinated disaster response and resilient building practices in the face of increasingly volatile weather patterns.

Background

India’s climate is dominated by the seasonal monsoon, which typically arrives in June and brings the bulk of annual rainfall. However, the pre‑monsoon period often experiences erratic weather, with periods of intense heat interspersed with isolated thunderstorms. In recent years, climate models have suggested a trend toward more pronounced temperature extremes and a higher frequency of low‑pressure systems in the Bay of Bengal, which can serve as breeding grounds for cyclones.

Historically, the south coast of Andhra Pradesh has been vulnerable to cyclonic activity, with notable storms such as Cyclone Phailin in 2013 causing widespread damage. The region’s geography—flat coastal plains backed by the Eastern Ghats—makes it susceptible to both storm surge and inland flooding. The current low‑pressure disturbance follows a pattern observed in the past decade, where early‑season depressions have intensified rapidly, catching communities off‑guard despite improved forecasting capabilities.

What happens next

Meteorologists expect the low‑pressure system to maintain its trajectory over the Bay of Bengal for the next 24 to 36 hours, gradually weakening as it encounters higher wind shear. Forecast models indicate that the potential cyclone could either intensify into a severe cyclonic storm before making landfall or dissipate if sea‑surface temperatures drop. The IMD will issue a cyclone advisory within the next six hours, outlining expected wind speeds, rainfall totals, and storm‑surge heights for affected districts.

State governments along the threatened coastline have begun mobilising disaster response teams, pre‑positioning food, medical supplies, and temporary shelters. In central regions, authorities continue to monitor heat‑related health risks, urging the public to stay hydrated and avoid outdoor activities during peak heat. The convergence of these weather events will be closely watched, with real‑time updates disseminated through television, radio, and digital platforms to keep citizens informed.
