Trump administration sues California over alleged non‑citizen voter data

The Trump administration has taken legal action against California for not releasing a USB drive with alleged non‑citizen voter information. The Department of Justice also warns of a separate suit over ballot mailing practices.

Latest developments

On Monday, a federal lawsuit was filed by the Trump administration against the state of California, accusing the state of withholding a USB memory device that allegedly contains records of individuals who voted in recent elections despite not being U.S. citizens. The complaint, filed in a Washington, D.C. court, claims that the data could reveal systematic irregularities in the state's voter rolls.

California officials have responded by stating that the request for the USB drive violates state privacy laws and that the data in question has not been independently verified. The state's Attorney General's office has indicated that it will review the legal arguments and consider a protective order if the court orders the device's release.

In a related development, the Department of Justice has issued a warning that it may file a separate lawsuit against California for sending absentee ballots to individuals who are not eligible to vote. The DOJ alleges that the state's election officials distributed mail‑in ballots to addresses linked to non‑citizen residents, potentially compromising the integrity of the election process.

Legal experts note that both actions represent an escalation in the ongoing dispute over election administration between the federal government and California, a state that has traditionally taken a more permissive approach to voter access. The lawsuits could set precedents for how election data is handled and what obligations states have to disclose such information.

The cases are expected to proceed through the federal court system, with initial hearings scheduled for later this month. Observers from both parties are preparing for a protracted legal battle that may reach the Supreme Court if lower‑court rulings are appealed.

Why it matters

The lawsuits highlight a growing partisan clash over election security and voter eligibility. For voters in California and across the United States, the outcome could affect how easily people can cast ballots, especially those who rely on mail‑in voting.

If the federal government succeeds in compelling California to release the USB data, it could lead to increased scrutiny of voter rolls nationwide. Conversely, a ruling in favor of California could reinforce state autonomy in managing election records and protect privacy interests.

The DOJ's threat to sue over ballot distribution raises concerns for election administrators who must balance broad voter participation with safeguards against fraud. The legal precedents set by these cases may influence future legislation at both state and federal levels, potentially reshaping the rules governing voter registration and absentee voting.

Background

California has long been a focal point in national debates about voting rights. The state adopted policies that expand early voting, mail‑in ballots, and same‑day registration, aiming to increase turnout among historically underrepresented groups. Critics argue that these policies can create opportunities for ineligible voting, while supporters contend they are essential for a healthy democracy.

The issue of non‑citizen voting has resurfaced after the 2024 presidential election, when several media outlets reported that a small number of individuals who were not U.S. citizens may have cast ballots in certain jurisdictions. While the numbers reported were minimal, the allegations have been used by federal officials to call for stricter verification processes.

The Trump administration, which took office in 2025, has prioritized election integrity as a central policy goal. This includes pursuing legal actions aimed at increasing transparency around voter data and challenging state practices that the administration views as lax.

What happens next

The federal court will first decide whether California must produce the USB drive. If the court orders its release, the data will be examined by independent auditors to assess the validity of the non‑citizen voting claims. Simultaneously, the DOJ may file its own suit regarding ballot mailing, prompting another round of judicial review.

Both cases are likely to generate appeals, potentially reaching the Supreme Court. In the meantime, election officials in California are expected to review their ballot distribution procedures to avoid further legal challenges.
