Delhi High Court Halts Aadhaar‑Based Biometric Attendance for Prosecutors

The Delhi High Court temporarily blocked a rule mandating Aadhaar‑based biometric attendance for public prosecutors. The decision highlights ongoing legal debates over privacy and administrative authority.

Latest developments

On Monday, a bench of the Delhi High Court issued a stay on the Ministry of Law and Justice's directive that public prosecutors record their daily presence using Aadhaar‑linked biometric devices. The order, originally circulated in early March, required all prosecutors to clock in and out through a system that captures fingerprint and iris data linked to their unique Aadhaar numbers. The court's intervention came after a petition filed by a senior advocate argued that the measure infringed on the right to privacy and lacked a clear statutory basis. The stay means that, for the time being, prosecutors will continue to use the existing attendance mechanisms without biometric verification.

The judges noted that the petition raised serious questions about the proportionality of the biometric requirement, especially given that the Supreme Court has previously affirmed privacy as a fundamental right. While the order does not strike down the policy entirely, it pauses its implementation pending a full hearing on the merits. The Ministry of Law and Justice has been asked to submit a detailed response within four weeks, outlining the legal justification for the biometric system and how it complies with existing data‑protection norms.

Legal experts observing the case say the ruling could set a precedent for other government agencies that have introduced Aadhaar‑based attendance or authentication mechanisms. The decision also aligns with recent judgments in which courts have scrutinized the use of biometric data without explicit consent. As the case proceeds, both the government and the legal community are expected to present arguments on the balance between administrative efficiency and individual privacy rights.

Why it matters

The stay has immediate relevance for legal professionals in Pakistan, where similar debates over biometric attendance are emerging in public institutions. Pakistani prosecutors and civil servants monitor developments in Indian courts to gauge how privacy jurisprudence might evolve in the sub‑continent. If the Delhi High Court ultimately finds the Aadhaar mandate unconstitutional, it could encourage Pakistani lawmakers to reconsider any plans to introduce comparable biometric systems without robust safeguards.

Beyond the professional sphere, the case touches on broader public concerns about the collection and storage of biometric data. Citizens in both countries have expressed unease about the potential for misuse of such sensitive information. The court's willingness to pause the policy signals that privacy challenges are being taken seriously at the highest judicial levels, offering a reference point for advocacy groups pushing for stronger data‑protection legislation in Pakistan.

Background

Aadhaar, India's national identity program, assigns a unique 12‑digit number to residents based on biometric and demographic data. Since its launch, the government has linked Aadhaar to a range of services, from banking to welfare distribution. In early 2024, the Ministry of Law and Justice issued a circular directing all public prosecutors to adopt an Aadhaar‑based biometric attendance system, arguing that it would improve accountability and reduce absenteeism.

The directive was met with resistance from the legal fraternity, which raised concerns about the lack of a clear legal framework governing the use of Aadhaar for internal administrative purposes. Critics also pointed out that the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India affirmed the right to privacy, making any mandatory biometric requirement subject to strict scrutiny. Prior to the Delhi High Court's intervention, several state courts had already examined similar issues, with mixed outcomes.

The petition that led to the stay was filed by a senior advocate on behalf of a group of prosecutors who claimed that the biometric requirement violated their constitutional rights and exposed them to potential data breaches. The petition also highlighted procedural lapses, such as the absence of a legislative mandate authorizing the use of Aadhaar for attendance tracking.

What happens next

The Delhi High Court will hear detailed arguments from the Ministry of Law and Justice and the petitioners over the next several weeks. After reviewing the submissions, the bench may either lift the stay, modify the order, or uphold it pending a final judgment. The outcome will likely influence pending cases in other jurisdictions where governments seek to expand the use of biometric verification for public employees.
