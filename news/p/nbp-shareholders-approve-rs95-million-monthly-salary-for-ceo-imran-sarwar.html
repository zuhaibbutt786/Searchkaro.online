NBP shareholders approve Rs9.5 million monthly salary for CEO Imran Sarwar

Latest developments

The National Bank of Pakistan (NBP) announced on Tuesday that its shareholders have approved a remuneration package for Chief Executive Officer Imran Sarwar that includes a monthly salary of Rs9.5 million. The package also provides a life‑insurance cover worth Rs30 million, the allocation of two official cars and other ancillary benefits. The resolution was passed at the bank’s annual general meeting, where shareholders voted in favour of the proposal after a detailed presentation by the board. According to the filing submitted to the Pakistan Stock Exchange, the salary figure translates to roughly $55,000 per month at current exchange rates, positioning the NBP chief among the highest‑paid executives in the country’s banking sector. The life‑insurance component, valued at Rs30 million, is intended to protect the CEO’s family in the event of his untimely death, while the car allowance covers the cost of two vehicles for official and personal use. Investors and analysts noted that the approval reflects NBP’s strong financial performance over the past fiscal year, which posted a net profit of Rs15 billion and a rise in its capital base. The bank’s board argued that competitive compensation is essential to retain top talent and to drive further growth in a market increasingly dominated by private banks and fintech entrants. However, the decision has drawn criticism from some quarters of civil society and opposition politicians who argue that such high remuneration is inappropriate for a state‑owned institution that receives government support. They contend that the funds could be redirected toward expanding credit to small and medium‑sized enterprises or improving digital banking services for underserved populations.

Why it matters

The approval of a Rs9.5 million monthly salary for the NBP chief has immediate implications for public perception of government‑linked enterprises. Many Pakistanis view state banks as custodians of public money, and any perception of excessive executive pay can erode trust, especially in a climate of rising inflation and cost‑of‑living pressures. From a corporate governance perspective, the vote signals that NBP’s shareholders—largely comprising the federal government and institutional investors—are willing to endorse market‑aligned compensation structures. This could set a precedent for other state‑owned entities, prompting them to review their own pay scales and benefit packages. The move may also influence talent acquisition in the banking sector. By offering a remuneration package comparable to private‑sector rivals, NBP hopes to attract and retain senior managers who might otherwise migrate to more lucrative roles abroad or in multinational banks.

Background

Founded in 1949, the National Bank of Pakistan is the country’s oldest commercial bank and a key instrument of the government’s financial policy. Over the decades, NBP has expanded its branch network to more than 1,400 locations and offers a range of services from retail banking to corporate finance. Historically, the remuneration of NBP’s top executives has been modest compared with private banks. Previous CEOs earned salaries in the range of Rs3‑4 million per month, with limited ancillary benefits. The recent increase follows a broader trend of salary revisions across several state‑run corporations as they seek to modernize operations and compete for skilled leadership. The decision comes at a time when the Pakistani rupee has weakened and the central bank is tightening monetary policy to curb inflation. NBP’s robust balance sheet, however, has allowed it to maintain profitability despite the challenging macroeconomic environment.

What happens next

The board will now implement the approved compensation package, with the salary increase expected to take effect from the start of the next fiscal quarter. Shareholders have pledged to monitor the impact of the higher pay on the bank’s performance and to report any concerns at the subsequent AGM. Civil society groups have indicated they may file petitions or request parliamentary oversight to ensure that the remuneration aligns with the public interest. The outcome of such actions could shape future compensation policies for other government‑owned firms.
