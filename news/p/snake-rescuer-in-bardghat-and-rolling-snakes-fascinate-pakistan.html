Snake rescuer in Bardghat and rolling snakes fascinate Pakistan

Durga Lamichhane, a municipal worker in Bardghat, has become known for rescuing snakes in his community. Meanwhile, researchers have documented a rolling snake that can lunge away from threats at speeds up to four metres per second.

Latest developments

Durga Lamichhane, a municipal employee in the remote town of Bardghat, has been featured in local media after repeatedly intervening to remove snakes from homes and public spaces. According to Ratopati, Lamichhane balances his official duties with the unofficial role of a snake rescuer, often carrying the reptiles to safe locations away from human settlements. His actions have drawn attention from residents who view him as a bridge between traditional fear of snakes and modern wildlife protection.

In a separate scientific report, researchers described a previously undocumented locomotion method in a small colubrid snake that propels itself by forming a loop and rolling away from predators. The study, highlighted by VICE and IFLScience, notes that the snake can achieve speeds of almost four metres per second, a rate comparable to a fast human jog. The rolling motion, likened to a cartwheel, allows the animal to escape danger quickly and may represent an evolutionary response to specific environmental pressures.

Both stories have gained traction on Pakistani social platforms, where users share videos of Lamichhane’s rescues alongside clips of the rolling snake’s rapid movements. The juxtaposition of a local hero and an exotic scientific discovery has sparked discussions about human‑snake interactions and the need for greater public awareness of reptile behavior.

Authorities in the Bardghat municipality have not issued an official statement, but local officials have expressed appreciation for Lamichhane’s voluntary efforts, noting that his work reduces the likelihood of snakebite incidents in the area.

Why it matters

Pakistan records a high number of snakebite cases each year, particularly in rural regions where medical facilities are limited. By safely relocating snakes, Lamichhane helps lower the risk of accidental bites, contributing to community health and safety. His visible involvement also challenges long‑standing myths that portray snakes solely as threats, encouraging a more balanced view of these animals.

The discovery of a rolling snake adds a new dimension to the scientific understanding of reptile locomotion. If similar species exist in South Asian habitats, the finding could inform future research on snake behavior in Pakistan’s diverse ecosystems. Public fascination with the unusual movement may also drive interest in wildlife education, prompting schools and NGOs to incorporate reptile biology into curricula.

Together, the local rescue efforts and the global scientific breakthrough highlight the intersection of everyday human experience with cutting‑edge research. They underscore the importance of coexistence strategies that protect both people and wildlife, a priority for policymakers aiming to reduce snakebite mortality while preserving biodiversity.

Background

Snakes are common across Pakistan, ranging from the venomous saw‑scaled viper to the non‑venomous rat snake. Cultural attitudes often blend reverence and fear, leading many communities to kill snakes on sight. Over the past decade, NGOs have promoted humane relocation as a safer alternative, but resources remain scarce in remote districts like Bardghat.

The rolling snake, identified by herpetologists as a new behavioral variant within the genus *Crotophis*, was first observed in a laboratory setting where the animal was startled by a simulated predator. The researchers noted that the snake forms a tight loop, pushes against the ground, and propels itself forward in a series of rapid rolls. This locomotion differs from the typical sidewinding or serpentine movement seen in most snakes and may provide an advantage in open, sandy environments where quick escape is essential.

Both stories reflect broader trends in wildlife reporting: local human‑wildlife interactions gaining media attention alongside scientific discoveries that capture the public imagination. In Pakistan, where snakebite remains a public health concern, such narratives can influence attitudes toward conservation and safety.

What happens next

Local authorities are expected to formalise support for community‑based snake rescue programs, potentially providing training and protective equipment for volunteers like Lamichhane. Meanwhile, the research team plans to conduct field studies to determine whether the rolling locomotion occurs in wild populations within South Asia, which could lead to further publications and conservation recommendations.
