Pakistan drainage crisis worsens as monsoon floods expose gaps

Monsoon rains have overwhelmed Pakistan's drainage networks, causing widespread flooding. Authorities are reviewing international solutions, including a UK road drainage project, to improve resilience.

Latest developments

The recent monsoon season has pushed Pakistan's drainage systems to the breaking point, with several cities reporting waterlogged streets, damaged homes and disrupted transport. Dawn reported a "drainage disaster" after heavy rains caused sewage overflows in Karachi and Lahore, forcing local councils to declare emergency measures. In response, the federal government has convened a task force to assess the state of urban drainage and to prioritize urgent repairs.

Meanwhile, officials in the United Kingdom have completed a pioneering roadside improvement scheme in Hampshire that integrates a Sustainable Urban Drainage System (SUDS) to capture stormwater at its source. The project, highlighted by the Daily Echo, involved installing permeable paving, underground storage tanks and vegetated swales along a busy arterial road in Fareham. Portsmouth News noted that the new system is expected to reduce storm overflow incidents by up to 40 percent during peak rainfall.

Pakistani engineers have begun studying the Hampshire model as a possible template for local adaptation. A delegation from the Capital Development Authority visited the Fareham site last week, observing the layout of the drainage channels and the monitoring equipment used to track water flow. The delegation plans to draft a set of recommendations that could be piloted in high‑risk districts of Islamabad and Rawalpindi.

The Ministry of Climate Change has also announced a budget allocation of 2.5 billion rupees for emergency drainage upgrades in flood‑prone zones. The funds will be used to clear clogged drains, install temporary retention basins and upgrade existing culverts. The ministry emphasized that these actions are short‑term fixes while a longer‑term national stormwater strategy is being developed.

Why it matters

For millions of Pakistanis, reliable drainage is a matter of daily safety and economic stability. When drains fail, streets become impassable, markets lose business, and health risks rise due to stagnant water breeding disease vectors. The recent floods have already displaced thousands and strained emergency services, highlighting the urgent need for resilient infrastructure.

Adopting proven techniques from abroad can shorten the learning curve and reduce costly trial‑and‑error. The Hampshire scheme demonstrates how integrating green infrastructure with traditional road works can manage runoff without requiring massive new tunnels. If similar measures are applied in Pakistani cities, they could lessen the impact of future monsoon events, protect property values and lower the financial burden of disaster relief. Moreover, improved drainage supports broader development goals, such as encouraging investment in urban renewal projects and enhancing public confidence in municipal services.

Background

Pakistan's drainage challenges date back to rapid urbanisation in the 1990s, when many cities expanded faster than their sewer networks could keep up. Over time, illegal connections, inadequate maintenance and encroachment on natural waterways have compounded the problem. Climate data shows that monsoon intensity has increased by roughly 15 percent over the past two decades, putting additional pressure on already overstretched systems.

Internationally, many countries have turned to Sustainable Urban Drainage Systems to address similar issues. The UK, for example, has promoted SUDS for over a decade, encouraging local councils to incorporate permeable surfaces, rain gardens and underground storage. The Fareham project is the first roadside implementation of this approach in Hampshire, aiming to capture runoff before it reaches the main sewer line. Its success is being monitored by the Environment Agency, which hopes to replicate the model in other vulnerable catchments.

What happens next

The Pakistani task force is expected to release a draft action plan within the next month, outlining pilot projects in three major cities. These pilots will likely feature a mix of permeable pavement, detention pits and real‑time water‑level sensors, mirroring elements of the Hampshire design. Funding for the pilots will come from the newly announced budget as well as potential loans from the Asian Development Bank, which has expressed interest in supporting climate‑resilient infrastructure.

Stakeholders anticipate that the first pilot sites could be operational before the next monsoon season, providing a practical test of how foreign drainage concepts perform under local conditions.
