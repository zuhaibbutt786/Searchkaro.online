Delhi Faces Severe Air Pollution as AQI Surpasses 300, New Restrictions Imposed

Delhi’s air quality has reached hazardous levels, with an AQI of 428. New vehicle restrictions and fines aim to curb pollution during the winter season.

Latest developments

Delhi, the capital of India, has entered a new phase of air quality crisis as the Air Quality Index (AQI) climbed to 428 on Thursday, the highest reading recorded in recent years. The spike coincided with a sudden drop in temperature and the onset of winter, conditions that trap pollutants close to the ground. The Indian Meteorological Department reported that particulate matter (PM2.5) levels exceeded 300 µg/m³, a figure that places the city in the 'hazardous' category. Residents were advised to stay indoors and use air purifiers, while health officials warned of increased respiratory risks. In response to the alarming readings, the Delhi administration activated its Winter Action Plan, a set of measures designed to curb vehicle emissions during the cold season. The plan, first implemented last year, has now been intensified under the new GRAP-2 (Ground Level Air Pollution Restrictions) guidelines. According to the AajTak report, the city will impose stricter limits on the number of vehicles allowed on the road, introduce higher parking charges, and enforce a 30% reduction in fuel consumption for private cars. The government also announced a temporary ban on the use of diesel generators in public spaces. The enforcement of these restrictions has already led to significant fines and vehicle returns. Dainik Bhaskar highlighted that over 1,500 vehicles were pulled back from the border and fined a total of ₹68.4 lakh on the first day of the plan. The fines target vehicles that exceed the permissible emissions standards or that are registered in the prohibited zones. The authorities also plan to increase roadside monitoring and deploy additional traffic police to ensure compliance. The impact of the high AQI extends beyond Delhi. India TV reported that Mumbai and 18 other states were also experiencing dense fog and storm alerts, creating a regional weather pattern that exacerbates air quality problems. The haze has reduced visibility, disrupted transportation, and forced schools and offices to close temporarily. The Ministry of Environment has urged all states to coordinate with Delhi in sharing data and best practices for pollution control. Experts note that the combination of industrial emissions, vehicular pollution, and seasonal weather conditions creates a perfect storm for Delhi’s air quality. The city’s annual average AQI has been rising steadily over the past decade, and the current spike is a clear indicator that existing measures are insufficient. The government’s focus on vehicle restrictions is a step in the right direction, but long-term solutions will require a shift towards cleaner energy sources and stricter industrial regulation.

Why it matters

For Delhi’s residents, the hazardous air quality poses immediate health risks. Children, the elderly, and people with pre-existing respiratory conditions are especially vulnerable to asthma attacks, bronchitis, and other illnesses. Hospitals have reported a surge in emergency admissions for breathing difficulties during the last week of November. Beyond health, the economic cost of pollution is mounting. Businesses report reduced productivity as workers miss days due to illness, and tourism has taken a hit as travelers avoid the city during peak pollution periods. The Delhi Metro, one of the city’s primary public transport systems, has seen a decline in ridership as commuters opt for home office arrangements. The government’s restrictions also affect daily life. Residents are required to adjust their travel plans, and many have reported difficulty accessing essential services during the curfew hours. The increased parking fees have added to the cost of living, especially for low-income households that rely on private vehicles for commuting.

Background

Delhi’s struggle with air pollution dates back to the early 2000s, when rapid urbanization and industrial growth began to outpace environmental safeguards. The city’s geography, surrounded by the Aravalli Range, traps pollutants and prevents dispersion, making it one of the most polluted capitals in the world. The Indian government introduced the National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) in 2019, setting a target to reduce PM2.5 levels by 20% by 2024. While progress has been made in some regions, Delhi’s levels remain stubbornly high, prompting the state to adopt more aggressive measures such as the Winter Action Plan. Previous iterations of vehicle restrictions, such as the 2015 Delhi Air Pollution Control Act, have shown mixed results. While they reduced traffic congestion, they did not significantly lower particulate matter concentrations, leading to the current focus on stricter enforcement and broader pollution controls.

What happens next

The Delhi administration plans to monitor AQI levels daily and adjust restrictions accordingly. If readings fall below 300, some restrictions may be eased, but the government has warned that the Winter Action Plan will remain in force until the end of January. Experts suggest that the city will need to invest in green infrastructure, such as tree planting and the expansion of electric public transport, to achieve long‑term improvements. The Ministry of Environment has announced a new funding package aimed at supporting such initiatives across the national capital.
