India Repatriates Over 60 Tonnes of Gold, Boosts Domestic Reserves

The RBI has recovered over 60 tonnes of gold from overseas vaults, pushing domestic holdings to 84% of the total reserve. The move signals a shift in India's gold‑storage policy.

Latest developments

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) announced that it has successfully repatriated more than 60 tonnes of gold from foreign custodians over the past six months, ending November 2026. The metal was transferred from vaults in Switzerland, the United Kingdom and other jurisdictions where India traditionally kept a sizeable portion of its reserves. The operation was carried out under a confidential agreement with overseas banks and involved meticulous logistics to ensure the safety and purity of the bullion.

Following the repatriation, the RBI disclosed that 84 percent of India’s total gold reserves are now stored within the country’s borders. The remaining 16 percent continues to be held abroad, primarily for liquidity and diversification purposes. The domestic stock is housed in a network of high‑security vaults operated by the RBI, including several locations that have not been publicly identified. Analysts note that the concentration of gold in Indian vaults reduces exposure to foreign exchange risk and aligns with the government’s broader goal of self‑reliance in strategic assets.

Market observers reported a modest rise in gold prices on Indian exchanges after the news broke, reflecting investor confidence in the country’s enhanced reserve position. While the repatriated gold does not directly affect the supply of bullion in the market, the perception of a stronger sovereign reserve base can influence currency stability and borrowing costs. The RBI’s move also comes at a time when global gold prices are volatile due to geopolitical tensions and shifting monetary policies in major economies.

Why it matters

India’s cultural affinity for gold makes the metal a key component of household wealth, but it also plays a strategic role in the nation’s financial stability. By increasing the share of gold held domestically, the RBI reduces the country’s dependence on foreign custodians, thereby limiting the impact of external policy changes or capital controls on its reserve assets. This shift can help safeguard the value of the rupee during periods of external pressure.

The repatriation also has fiscal implications. Importing gold has historically contributed to a sizable trade deficit, and the government has been encouraging domestic sourcing and recycling to curb the outflow of foreign exchange. With more gold stored at home, the RBI can potentially use the metal as collateral for sovereign borrowing, offering a low‑cost financing option that does not strain the fiscal budget.

For investors, the news reinforces confidence in India’s macro‑economic management. A larger domestic gold pool can act as a buffer against currency depreciation, which is particularly relevant for exporters and foreign investors monitoring the rupee’s trajectory. The move may also influence the pricing of gold‑linked financial products, such as exchange‑traded funds and sovereign gold bonds, by signalling a more secure underlying asset base.

Background

India has long been one of the world’s largest consumers of gold, with cultural traditions driving demand for jewelry, festivals and weddings. Historically, the RBI kept a substantial portion of its gold reserves in overseas vaults to facilitate international settlements and to diversify storage risk. Over the past decade, however, policymakers have voiced concerns about the vulnerability of foreign‑held reserves to geopolitical shocks and currency fluctuations.

In 2020, the RBI began a gradual policy shift, encouraging the return of gold to domestic vaults as part of a broader strategy to enhance financial sovereignty. The initiative was complemented by the launch of sovereign gold bonds, which offered investors a government‑backed alternative to physical gold purchases. These bonds have helped channel private gold demand into the formal financial system, reducing the pressure on imports.

The recent repatriation builds on this trajectory. By moving more than 60 tonnes of gold back to India, the RBI has accelerated the timeline for achieving a predominantly domestic reserve composition. The operation reflects improvements in the country’s logistical capabilities, including secure transport corridors and advanced vault technology that meet international safety standards.

What happens next

The RBI is expected to continue its repatriation programme, targeting additional batches of gold held abroad in the coming fiscal year. Officials have hinted at the possibility of expanding domestic storage capacity, potentially through partnerships with private vault operators that meet stringent security criteria. Further transparency about the locations and quantities of stored gold may be released in future quarterly reports.

In parallel, the government is likely to promote policies that encourage the recycling of gold from old jewellery and electronic waste, feeding the domestic reserve pool without increasing imports. Such measures could reinforce the trend of a self‑sufficient gold strategy and support the broader goal of reducing the trade deficit.
