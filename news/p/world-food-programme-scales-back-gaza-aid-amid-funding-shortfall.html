World Food Programme Scales Back Gaza Aid Amid Funding Shortfall

The United Nations' World Food Programme announced a reduction in food assistance for half a million Palestinians in the Gaza Strip after donor contributions fell short. The cuts raise urgent concerns about rising hunger and the humanitaria

Latest developments

The United Nations World Food Programme (WFP) disclosed on Wednesday that it will reduce its food assistance programme in the Gaza Strip, affecting roughly 500,000 Palestinians. The decision follows a sharp decline in voluntary contributions, leaving the agency with a funding gap that threatens its ability to maintain existing operations. According to the WFP's own release, the shortfall stems from delayed pledges by several donor governments and a slowdown in private sector funding.

The cut means that the number of beneficiaries receiving monthly food parcels will be halved, and the daily ration per person will drop from the current 2,100 calories to around 1,500 calories. WFP officials warned that the reduction could push many families below the minimum nutritional threshold, especially those already displaced or living in densely populated camps.

Donor representatives have expressed concern over the funding crunch. A spokesperson for the European Commission said that budgetary pressures in member states have limited the ability to meet previously pledged amounts. Meanwhile, several Gulf states are reviewing their contributions, and the United States has indicated a possible re‑allocation of funds to other emergency responses in the region.

Humanitarian partners on the ground, including the United Nations Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA) and local NGOs, are scrambling to fill the gap. They have begun emergency cash‑for‑food distributions and are urging the international community to mobilise additional resources before the situation deteriorates further.

Why it matters

For readers in Pakistan, the reduction in Gaza aid resonates on several levels. Pakistan has historically supported the Palestinian cause, and many citizens follow developments in the Gaza Strip closely. A worsening humanitarian crisis could spur public calls for the Pakistani government and private donors to increase their contributions to relief efforts.

The food insecurity in Gaza also has broader implications for regional stability. Escalating hardship can fuel displacement flows, potentially increasing the number of refugees seeking asylum in neighboring countries, including those with existing refugee populations from Gaza. This scenario may affect Pakistan's diplomatic engagements and its role in international humanitarian forums.

Moreover, the funding shortfall highlights the vulnerability of aid systems that rely heavily on voluntary contributions. Pakistani NGOs that operate in conflict zones may face similar challenges, underscoring the need for diversified and reliable financing mechanisms to sustain long‑term assistance.

Background

The Gaza Strip, a narrow coastal enclave home to about two million people, has been under an Israeli‑imposed blockade since 2007. Repeated rounds of conflict have devastated infrastructure, limited access to essential services, and created chronic food shortages. The United Nations estimates that a significant portion of Gaza's population depends on external food aid to meet basic dietary needs.

Since the escalation of hostilities in 2023, the WFP has been one of the largest providers of food assistance in the territory, delivering monthly food parcels and operating cash‑based programmes to support vulnerable families. Prior to the recent funding cuts, the agency was able to reach roughly one million individuals, covering both displaced households and those living in permanent residences.

Funding for the WFP's Gaza operations traditionally comes from a mix of government pledges, private donations, and contributions from multilateral institutions. However, global economic pressures and competing humanitarian emergencies have strained donor budgets, leading to delayed or reduced payments for the Gaza programme.

What happens next

The WFP has launched an urgent appeal for $150 million to restore its full assistance package in Gaza. The agency hopes to secure new pledges before the end of the year, emphasizing that without additional resources, the number of people facing acute food insecurity could rise sharply.

In parallel, the United Nations is reviewing its coordination mechanisms to ensure that remaining funds are allocated efficiently. Stakeholders anticipate that if the appeal falls short, further reductions in food rations or a shift to more targeted cash assistance may become necessary.
