Houthi missile strike damages Riyadh’s tallest PIF Tower

A Houthi missile hit the PIF Tower, the tallest building in Riyadh and home to the Saudi sovereign wealth fund. Saudi-led air defenses shot down several other missiles aimed at the capital.

Latest developments

The Public Investment Fund (PIF) Tower, a 385‑metre, 71‑storey skyscraper that dominates Riyadh’s skyline, was hit by a missile launched from Houthi‑controlled Yemen on Monday. According to Reuters, the projectile struck the tower’s upper façade, causing visible structural damage and prompting an immediate evacuation of the building’s occupants. The PIF Tower houses the headquarters of Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund, making it a symbolic target in the broader regional conflict.

Saudi Arabia’s air defence network responded swiftly. The Saudi‑led coalition reported that its Patriot and Arrow systems intercepted a number of additional missiles that were launched in the same wave toward the capital and the southern provinces. Al Jazeera confirmed that the coalition’s forces successfully neutralised the majority of the incoming threats, preventing further damage to critical infrastructure.

Initial assessments from on‑site engineers indicate that the impact has compromised the tower’s external cladding and may have affected internal utilities on the affected floors. No casualties have been reported, and emergency services have been on standby to assist any injured personnel. The Saudi Ministry of Interior released a brief statement saying that the situation is under control and that investigations are underway to determine the exact origin and trajectory of the missile.

International reaction has been swift. The United Nations Security Council is expected to convene an emergency session to discuss the escalation of hostilities, while the United States has reiterated its support for Saudi Arabia’s right to self‑defence. The incident marks the first time the PIF Tower, a symbol of the kingdom’s economic diversification under Vision 2030, has been directly targeted.

The attack also raised concerns about the security of other high‑rise projects in the Gulf region, prompting several construction firms to review their risk assessments and emergency response protocols.

Why it matters

For readers in Pakistan, the strike underscores the volatility of the broader Middle East theatre, where any escalation can have ripple effects on regional energy markets. Saudi Arabia is a key oil exporter, and disruptions to its production or transport infrastructure can influence global oil prices, which in turn affect Pakistan’s import bills and balance of payments.

The PIF Tower is not just an office building; it represents Saudi Arabia’s ambitious push to diversify its economy away from oil through massive investments in real estate, technology, and tourism. Damage to such a flagship project could signal vulnerabilities in the kingdom’s economic reform agenda, potentially impacting foreign investment flows that Pakistani businesses monitor closely.

Moreover, the incident highlights the growing reach of Houthi missile capabilities, which have been increasingly used to target high‑value assets deep inside Saudi territory. This development may prompt neighboring countries, including Pakistan, to reassess their own defence postures and diplomatic strategies toward the conflict, especially given the historic ties between Pakistan and Saudi Arabia.

Background

The Houthi movement, based in Yemen’s northern provinces, has been engaged in a protracted civil war with the internationally recognised Yemeni government since 2014. Backed by Iran, the Houthis have launched a series of missile and drone attacks against Saudi Arabia since the coalition’s intervention in 2015, aiming to pressure the kingdom over its role in the Yemeni conflict.

Riyadh’s PIF Tower, completed in 2022, is the tallest structure in the Saudi capital and serves as the headquarters of the Public Investment Fund, which manages assets worth over $600 billion. The tower’s construction was a centerpiece of Vision 2030, a reform plan introduced by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman to modernise the Saudi economy.

In recent months, the coalition has intensified its air‑defence deployments along the Saudi‑Yemen border, integrating advanced missile‑interception systems supplied by the United States and European allies. Despite these measures, the Houthis have demonstrated an ability to strike deep into Saudi territory, as seen in previous attacks on oil facilities and airports.

What happens next

Saudi authorities are expected to conduct a thorough forensic investigation of the missile remnants to confirm the weapon type and launch coordinates. In parallel, the coalition will likely boost its aerial patrols and surface‑to‑air missile readiness around key urban centres.

Diplomatic channels are also anticipated to remain active, with the United Nations and regional powers urging restraint to prevent further escalation that could destabilise the already fragile security environment in the Gulf.
