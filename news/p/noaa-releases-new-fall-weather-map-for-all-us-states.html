NOAA releases new fall weather map for all U.S. states

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration unveiled a detailed fall weather map for the United States. The graphic highlights regional temperature shifts and precipitation expectations as summer ends.

What is happening

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) released a new interactive map that visualizes projected temperature and precipitation patterns for the fall months of September through November. The map divides the United States into climate zones and assigns color‑coded bands to indicate whether a region is likely to experience above‑average warmth, cooler conditions, or near‑average temperatures. Precipitation forecasts are similarly displayed, showing areas where rain or early snowfall may be more common than in a typical year.

The tool is accessible through NOAA’s public website and can be viewed on desktop or mobile devices. Users can toggle between temperature and precipitation layers, zoom into individual states, and compare the current forecast with historical averages. The map also includes a brief narrative that explains the main drivers of the seasonal outlook, such as lingering summer heat, jet‑stream positioning, and oceanic temperature anomalies.

Meteorologists and emergency managers are encouraged to incorporate the map into their planning processes. For example, agricultural advisors may use the temperature bands to advise farmers on crop‑selection timing, while municipal officials can anticipate potential flooding or early snow removal needs. The map is updated weekly as new model runs become available, ensuring that the information reflects the latest atmospheric data.

Public reaction has been active, with many residents sharing the map on social media to gauge how their local weather may change. News outlets across the country have referenced the graphic in their seasonal forecasts, and weather‑related search queries have spiked in the days following the release.

As summer draws to a close, Americans are increasingly interested in what the cooler months will bring. The transition from hot, humid conditions to milder temperatures affects travel plans, outdoor activities, and energy consumption, prompting a surge in online searches for reliable forecasts.

The timing of NOAA’s release coincides with a period of heightened media coverage about extreme weather events earlier in the year, such as heatwaves and severe storms. Readers are looking for authoritative sources that can help them understand whether similar patterns might continue into the fall. The map’s state‑by‑state detail satisfies a demand for localized information rather than broad national summaries.

These broadcasts often include direct links to the NOAA page, driving traffic and search activity.

Finally, the agricultural sector is preparing for planting and harvest cycles that depend on seasonal weather. Farmers and agribusinesses frequently turn to NOAA’s outlooks to make decisions about irrigation, planting dates, and crop protection, further contributing to the search volume.

Background and context

NOAA’s seasonal outlooks are produced using a combination of global climate models, historical weather records, and real‑time observations from satellites and ground stations. The agency integrates these data streams to generate probabilistic forecasts that estimate the likelihood of temperature and precipitation deviations from long‑term averages.

Historically, fall in the United States has shown considerable variability. Some years bring an early onset of cooler air masses, while others experience extended warmth that can affect ecosystems and human activities. NOAA’s seasonal maps aim to capture this variability by presenting a range of possible outcomes rather than a single deterministic prediction.

The current map reflects the influence of several large‑scale climate drivers, including the El Niño‑Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Arctic Oscillation. These patterns can shift the jet stream, altering storm tracks and temperature gradients across the continent. By accounting for these drivers, NOAA improves the reliability of its seasonal guidance.

Public access to these forecasts has grown over the past decade as the agency has expanded its digital platforms. Interactive tools, such as the one released this fall, are part of a broader effort to make complex climate information understandable to non‑specialists.

The release also follows a series of congressional appropriations that increased funding for climate monitoring and forecasting, underscoring the importance placed on accurate seasonal predictions for public safety and economic planning.

What to know next

The map will be refreshed weekly for the remainder of the fall season, incorporating the latest model outputs and observational data. Users should check back regularly if they need the most current outlook for their region.

Beyond the map, NOAA plans to issue detailed regional bulletins that provide guidance on specific hazards, such as the potential for early frost in agricultural zones or heightened flood risk in river basins. These bulletins will be distributed through the agency’s email alerts and partner weather services.

Stakeholders, including city planners, school districts, and utility companies, are advised to integrate the seasonal outlook into their operational plans. By aligning resource allocation with the forecasted conditions, they can reduce the impact of unexpected weather events and improve service continuity.
