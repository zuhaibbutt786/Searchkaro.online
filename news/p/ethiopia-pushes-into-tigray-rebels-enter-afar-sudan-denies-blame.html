Ethiopia pushes into Tigray, rebels enter Afar; Sudan denies blame

Ethiopian troops are advancing further into Tigray as rebel forces make gains in the Afar region. Sudan has denied accusations of backing the rebels, sparking diplomatic friction.

Latest developments

The Ethiopian National Defense Force reported that its units have moved further into the interior of the Tigray region, reaching locations that have not been under government control since the conflict began. The advance follows a series of operations launched in early September and is described by officials as a strategic step to restore authority over the area. Military spokespeople said the troops are encountering resistance but are making steady progress, and they highlighted the need to secure supply routes that link the capital Addis Ababa with the northern front.

At the same time, fighters from the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) have pushed westward into the neighboring Afar region. According to reports from Euronews Arabic, the rebels entered Afar towns after a brief clash with local security forces, signalling a widening of the battlefield. The TPLF claim the move is intended to pressure the Ethiopian government and to open a new front that could force negotiations. Local authorities in Afar have called for additional federal support to repel the incursion.

Sudan’s army issued a statement denying accusations from Addis Ababa that it is supplying arms or logistical aid to the Tigray rebels. The Sudanese defence ministry described the claims as unfounded and warned that such allegations could damage the already fragile relations between the two countries. The statement came after Ethiopian officials publicly suggested that neighbouring states, including Sudan, might be assisting the insurgents.

The governor of Afar, who also serves as a senior regional commander, warned that Eritrean forces are operating alongside Ethiopian troops in the contested zones. He said the combined presence of Eritrean generals and weapons poses a significant challenge to any attempt at a cease‑fire. The governor called for a coordinated response to prevent further escalation and protect civilian populations caught in the crossfire.

Why it matters

Developments in the Horn of Africa have direct implications for South Africa’s foreign policy and trade interests. Instability in Ethiopia and its neighbours can disrupt key export routes for South African commodities, particularly those destined for Middle Eastern markets. Moreover, the risk of a broader regional conflict could affect South African companies operating in the area, ranging from mining firms to logistics providers.

Humanitarian concerns are also paramount. The United Nations estimates that millions of people remain displaced in Tigray and surrounding regions, with limited access to food, water and medical care. South African NGOs and diaspora groups have been active in delivering aid, and any escalation could increase the demand for international assistance, potentially prompting a larger contribution from South African donors.

Finally, the political dynamics may influence South Africa’s role in African Union peace‑building initiatives. As a prominent AU member, Pretoria is often called upon to mediate or support peace talks. A clear understanding of the latest military movements helps policymakers decide whether to push for diplomatic engagement or to back sanctions aimed at curbing external involvement.

Background

The conflict in Tigray began in November 2020 when the Ethiopian federal government launched a military operation against the Tigray People’s Liberation Front, accusing it of attacking a federal military base. The fighting quickly escalated, drawing in forces from neighboring Eritrea and leading to widespread reports of atrocities and a humanitarian crisis.

Over the past three years, control of major towns has shifted repeatedly between government and rebel forces. International observers have documented violations of international law on both sides, and several rounds of cease‑fire talks have collapsed. The involvement of external actors, including alleged support from Sudan and the presence of Eritrean troops, has added layers of complexity to an already volatile situation.

Afar, a low‑lying region bordering Ethiopia’s capital, has traditionally been a strategic corridor for trade and military movements. Its proximity to both Tigray and the Sudanese border makes it a focal point for any spill‑over of hostilities. The recent rebel advance into Afar marks the first major incursion of TPLF forces into the region since the conflict intensified.

What happens next

The United Nations and the African Union are expected to convene emergency meetings in the coming days to assess the security situation and explore options for a renewed cease‑fire. Diplomatic channels between Addis Ababa, Khartoum and Asmara are likely to be tested as each side seeks to protect its strategic interests.

If the fighting continues to spread, the international community may consider expanding sanctions on entities that provide military assistance to any party. Conversely, a swift diplomatic push could lead to a temporary pause in hostilities, allowing humanitarian corridors to be re‑opened for aid delivery.
