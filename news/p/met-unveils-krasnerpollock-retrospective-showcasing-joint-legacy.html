Met Unveils Krasner‑Pollock Retrospective Showcasing Joint Legacy

New York's Metropolitan Museum launches a joint retrospective of Lee Krasner and Jackson Pollock. The show aims to reposition Krasner’s work alongside her famous partner, sparking conversation among art lovers in Pakistan.

Latest developments

The Metropolitan Museum of Art opened its new exhibition "Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous" on Tuesday, presenting more than 80 paintings, drawings and archival materials that pair the late abstract expressionists side by side. Curated by veteran art historian Dr. Sarah Green, the show is organized around the themes of love, pain and artistic dialogue, reflecting the complex personal and creative bond between the two artists.

Major U.S. publications have praised the exhibition for its balanced narrative. The New York Times highlighted how the pairing reveals the emotional intensity that powered both artists' work, while the Wall Street Journal called the display "the power couple of abstract painting" and noted its fresh perspective on their intertwined careers. Artsy’s editorial team added that the show finally steps Lee Krasner out of Jackson Pollock’s shadow, giving her a distinct platform within the museum’s galleries.

Among the highlighted pieces are Krasner’s 1955 canvas "The Seasons," a vibrant composition that critics say demonstrates her command of color and form, and Pollock’s iconic "Number 31" (1950), whose swirling layers dominate the central hall. The exhibition also includes personal letters, photographs and a rarely seen sketchbook that document the couple’s artistic exchange during the 1940s and 1950s.

Visitor numbers have surged since the opening, with the museum reporting a 30 percent increase in attendance compared with the same period last year. Social media chatter from Pakistan, especially on platforms like Twitter and Instagram, shows a growing fascination with the exhibition, as users share photos and discuss the relevance of abstract expressionism to contemporary South Asian art.

The Met has scheduled a series of related events, including a lecture by Pakistani art historian Dr. Ayesha Khan on the influence of Western abstract art in South Asia, a family‑friendly workshop on gesture painting, and a virtual tour designed for schools across Pakistan. These programs aim to broaden the exhibition’s reach beyond New York’s museum-goers.

Why it matters

For readers in Pakistan, the exhibition offers a rare glimpse into the formative years of abstract expressionism, a movement that continues to shape local artistic practice. Pakistani painters and students can see how personal narratives—love, loss, and resilience—can be translated into non‑representational forms, encouraging them to explore similar emotional depth in their own work.

The Met’s decision to foreground Krasner alongside Pollock also resonates with ongoing discussions about gender equity in the arts. By presenting her oeuvre as equal in stature, the museum provides a model for Pakistani institutions seeking to highlight women artists who have been historically overlooked. This shift may inspire curators in Karachi and Lahore to re‑examine their own collections for under‑represented voices.

Background

Lee Krasner and Jackson Pollock met in the early 1940s in New York’s burgeoning avant‑garde scene. Both became central figures in the abstract expressionist movement, yet Pollock’s fame quickly eclipsed Krasner’s, partly due to his dramatic public persona and the tragic circumstances of his death in 1956. Over the decades, critics have reassessed Krasner’s contributions, noting her sophisticated use of color and her role in shaping Pollock’s artistic direction.

Previous retrospectives have tended to treat Krasner as a footnote to Pollock’s legacy. The 2022 exhibition at the Whitney Museum began to challenge that narrative, but the Met’s current show is the most comprehensive pairing to date, offering new scholarship and previously unseen works. The exhibition’s title, "Past Continuous," references the ongoing dialogue between the two artists, suggesting that their creative exchange was never truly finished.

What happens next

The Met plans to loan select works from the Krasner‑Pollock show to the National Museum of Fine Arts in Islamabad later next year, creating a direct cultural bridge between the United States and Pakistan. In addition, a traveling version of the exhibition is slated for major Asian cities, including Tokyo and Seoul, before returning to the United States for a final run in 2027.
