UK faces warm September spell with temperatures near 28°C

The Met Office has issued a warning that parts of the United Kingdom could see temperatures close to 28°C in September. The unusual warmth is prompting public health advice and heightened interest online.

What is happening

The Met Office has released a forecast indicating that several regions of the United Kingdom will experience daytime highs approaching 28°C during the second half of September. This temperature level is well above the seasonal average for the month and has triggered a yellow heat‑health alert for the affected areas. The forecast covers a period of several days, with the peak expected on a mid‑week day when high pressure will dominate the weather pattern.

Southern England, including counties such as Kent, Sussex and Hampshire, is expected to be among the warmest zones. Wales and parts of the Midlands are also forecast to see temperatures above 27°C, creating a band of warmth that stretches from the south coast up through central England. The Met Office’s regional maps highlight seven distinct areas where the temperature threshold of 27°C will be breached, a level that is rarely seen at this time of year.

The underlying cause of the warm spell is a strong high‑pressure system that has settled over western Europe. This ridge of high pressure is drawing warm, dry air from the continent and the Atlantic, suppressing cloud formation and allowing solar radiation to heat the surface efficiently. The clear skies and light winds further enhance daytime heating, while nighttime temperatures are expected to remain relatively mild, reducing the typical diurnal cooling that usually moderates September weather.

Authorities are monitoring the situation because sustained warmth can increase demand for cooling equipment, raise the risk of heat‑related illness, and place additional load on the electricity network. While the heat is not expected to reach the extremes seen in summer, health agencies have issued guidance on staying hydrated, limiting strenuous outdoor activity during peak heat, and checking on vulnerable individuals such as the elderly and young children.

Public interest spikes whenever weather deviates from the norm, and the prospect of a September heatwave has prompted a surge in online searches. Residents are looking for up‑to‑date temperature forecasts, advice on how to stay comfortable, and information on any official warnings that may affect daily routines.

Travel plans and outdoor events are also a factor. With schools and workplaces often scheduling activities for early autumn, people want to know whether the warm spell might affect transportation, sports fixtures, or community gatherings. Social media posts featuring unusually warm street scenes further amplify curiosity, driving more users to seek reliable data from the Met Office and other weather services.

Background and context

Historically, September in the United Kingdom sees average maximum temperatures ranging from 15°C to 20°C, depending on the region. Temperatures above 25°C are considered warm for the month, and readings near 28°C are uncommon, occurring only a handful of times in the past few decades. Climate records show a gradual upward trend in late‑summer and early‑autumn temperatures, reflecting broader changes in regional climate patterns.

The Met Office operates a tiered heat‑health alert system that ranges from yellow (caution) to red (danger). A yellow alert, which has been issued for the current warm spell, signals that temperatures are high enough to pose a risk to health, especially for vulnerable groups. The alert system is designed to prompt local authorities and health services to implement preventative measures, such as opening cooling centres and issuing public health messages.

Previous warm spells in September, such as those recorded in 2016 and 2022, have been linked to similar high‑pressure setups. Those events provided valuable lessons on how to manage public services during atypical heat, leading to improved coordination between meteorological agencies, health officials, and community organisations.

What to know next

The Met Office will continue to update its forecasts on an hourly basis as the high‑pressure system evolves. Residents should monitor official channels for any changes to the heat‑health alert level, especially if temperatures are projected to climb further or if the spell extends beyond the initial forecast period.

Health agencies advise practical steps: drink plenty of water, avoid alcohol and caffeine during the hottest parts of the day, wear light clothing, and seek shade or air‑conditioned environments when possible. Employers are encouraged to adjust break times for outdoor workers and to provide cooling facilities where feasible. Keeping an eye on local news will ensure that any additional guidance, such as temporary closures of vulnerable‑population shelters, is received promptly.
