US FDA to Revise Infant Formula Standards After 30‑Year Gap

Latest developments

On Thursday, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) released a draft proposal to revise the nutrient limits and labeling requirements for infant formula. The proposal, which follows a nearly 30‑year pause in regulatory changes, seeks to align U.S. standards with recent scientific evidence on infant nutrition. It would introduce stricter limits on certain nutrients such as iron, zinc and added sugars, while also expanding the information that manufacturers must provide on product labels.

Industry groups have responded with mixed signals. The International Dairy Federation said the changes would encourage innovation but raised concerns about the cost of reformulation for smaller companies. A spokesperson for the American Association of Food Science and Technology noted that the proposal would require manufacturers to conduct additional testing to demonstrate compliance with the new limits.

The proposed rules could reshape the infant formula market in the United States and abroad. Consumers would see clearer labeling that indicates the presence of added sugars and the exact amounts of key nutrients. Some analysts predict that the changes may drive a shift toward fortified or specialty formulas that meet the new criteria.

Globally, the FDA’s move follows similar efforts by the European Food Safety Authority and the World Health Organization, which have updated their own guidelines in recent years. The U.S. proposal is expected to influence international trade, as U.S. manufacturers export a significant share of their products to markets such as Pakistan, where infant formula is a common dietary supplement for newborns.

Why it matters

For parents in Pakistan, the FDA’s updated standards mean that infant formula sold in the U.S. and subsequently imported will carry more detailed nutrition information. This can help caregivers make better choices about which products meet the nutritional needs of their children. The changes also aim to reduce the risk of over‑supplementation of certain minerals that can be harmful to infants.

The revised labeling rules will require manufacturers to list the amounts of added sugars and other additives in grams per serving. Pakistani regulators may use the U.S. guidelines as a benchmark when reviewing local infant formula products, potentially leading to tighter domestic regulations and improved consumer protection.

Background

Infant formula has been regulated by the FDA since 1958, with the last major update in 1997. The current proposal marks the first significant revision in almost three decades, reflecting new research on infant growth patterns and the role of micronutrients. The draft proposal was released to invite public comment, a process that typically lasts 60 days.

The proposal also addresses concerns raised by health advocates about the presence of added sugars in some formulas. By setting clearer limits, the FDA intends to reduce the likelihood of excessive sugar intake in early childhood, which is linked to obesity and dental problems later in life.

What happens next

The FDA will review comments received during the public comment period and may adjust the proposal before finalizing the rule. Once adopted, the new standards will apply to all infant formula manufactured or imported into the United States. Manufacturers will have a transition period to reformulate products and update labels.
