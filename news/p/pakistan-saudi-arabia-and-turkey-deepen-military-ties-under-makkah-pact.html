Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey deepen military ties under Makkah pact

Military chiefs from Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey gathered in Riyadh to chart deeper cooperation under the Makkah defence pact. The talks hint at joint action against the Houthi insurgency and a possible broader Muslim‑world security f

Latest developments

Senior military leaders from Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey convened in Riyadh on Monday to review the Makkah defence alliance, a trilateral security framework signed earlier this year. The chiefs exchanged views on how Islamabad and Ankara could assist Riyadh in its ongoing campaign against the Houthi rebels in Yemen, a conflict that has drawn increasing Saudi attention. While no formal deployment was announced, the participants agreed to explore logistical and intelligence support, signalling a willingness to move beyond diplomatic statements.

In parallel, Turkish officials indicated that Ankara is prepared to provide air‑defence assets and advisory teams if Saudi Arabia requests direct assistance. Pakistani military representatives echoed this stance, noting that Pakistan's experience in counter‑insurgency could be valuable for operations in the Red Sea corridor. Both countries stressed that any involvement would be contingent on a clear request from the Saudi government and would respect existing international obligations.

A Turkish parliamentary speaker, speaking to Reuters, suggested that the Makkah pact could evolve into a broader coalition that includes other Muslim‑majority states, potentially even Iran, if regional security concerns align. This comment reflects a growing ambition among the three signatories to position the alliance as a pan‑Islamic security mechanism rather than a bilateral arrangement.

Analysts note that the timing of the meeting coincides with heightened tensions in the Gulf, where the Houthi missile and drone attacks on Saudi oil facilities have intensified. The trilateral dialogue therefore serves both a strategic and a signaling purpose, demonstrating to regional adversaries that Saudi Arabia has the backing of two additional military powers.

Why it matters

For Pakistan, deeper engagement with the Makkah defence pact offers an avenue to strengthen its strategic partnership with Saudi Arabia, a key economic ally that hosts a large expatriate community. Enhanced military cooperation could translate into greater investment and trade opportunities, which are especially significant given Pakistan's current fiscal challenges.

Turkey views the alliance as a means to reassert its influence in the Middle East after years of diplomatic friction with several Gulf states. By aligning with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, Ankara hopes to showcase its role as a security guarantor for the broader Muslim world, a narrative that resonates with its domestic audience.

In the Pakistani public sphere, the prospect of involvement in a Saudi‑led operation against the Houthis raises debates about the country's foreign policy direction and the potential risks of entanglement in a protracted regional conflict. Meanwhile, Saudi Arabia gains diplomatic weight by presenting a united front with two prominent Muslim nations, which could deter further Houthi aggression and reassure domestic constituencies.

Background

The Makkah defence pact was signed in early 2026 as a trilateral agreement aimed at fostering security cooperation among Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey. Named after the holy city of Makkah, the pact emphasizes mutual assistance in the face of external threats to the Muslim world. Since its inception, the alliance has focused on joint training exercises, intelligence sharing and the development of interoperable command structures.

Saudi Arabia has been leading a coalition against the Houthi movement in Yemen since 2015, a war that has strained its military resources and exposed vulnerabilities in its air‑defence network. Over the years, Riyadh has sought additional partners to share the operational burden, prompting outreach to both Islamabad and Ankara.

Both Pakistan and Turkey have historic ties with Saudi Arabia, rooted in religious, economic and political connections. Their militaries have previously collaborated on humanitarian missions and counter‑terrorism initiatives, laying a foundation for the more formalized cooperation outlined in the Makkah pact.

What happens next

The three governments are expected to issue joint statements in the coming days outlining concrete steps for intelligence exchange and logistical coordination. A series of bilateral meetings between defence ministries may lead to the drafting of a memorandum of understanding that specifies the scope of any future deployment. Observers anticipate that the next phase could include joint naval patrols in the Red Sea to protect shipping lanes from Houthi missile threats.
