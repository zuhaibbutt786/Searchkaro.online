Latest developments

In parallel, the central bank intervened in the foreign exchange market, selling dollars from its reserves to support the rupee. Market participants noted that the dollar's upward momentum was driven by a combination of higher global oil prices, a stronger US economy, and renewed capital outflows from emerging markets. Domestic banks reported a surge in demand for foreign currency, especially among import‑dependent firms seeking to lock in rates before further declines. The SBP also reminded commercial banks to adhere to prudential guidelines on foreign exchange exposure, warning that excessive speculation could exacerbate volatility. While the rupee’s slide has sparked concerns, some analysts point to a narrowing current‑account deficit and steady remittance inflows as factors that could stabilize the situation over the medium term.

Why it matters

A weaker rupee directly raises the cost of imported goods, from essential fuel to food items, which in turn pushes consumer prices higher. Inflation, already above the SBP's target range, is expected to edge closer to double digits if the currency continues to lose ground. Households feel the impact through higher utility bills and increased prices for everyday commodities. For businesses, especially those reliant on imported raw materials, the rising dollar adds to production costs, potentially squeezing profit margins and leading to higher prices for finished goods. The banking sector also faces heightened risk as loan portfolios with foreign‑currency exposure become more vulnerable. Moreover, the depreciation affects Pakistan's external debt service obligations, which are largely denominated in dollars, increasing the fiscal burden and potentially limiting the government's ability to fund social programs. Finally, the currency trend influences foreign investor sentiment, as a volatile exchange rate can deter new capital inflows and affect existing portfolio investments.

Background

The relationship between the US dollar and the Pakistani rupee has been marked by periodic turbulence over the past two decades. Following the 2008 global financial crisis, the rupee depreciated sharply as foreign reserves fell and external borrowing surged. A more recent episode in 2018 saw the rupee slide past 130 per dollar, prompting the SBP to raise interest rates dramatically to defend the currency. Historically, Pakistan has relied on a mix of foreign direct investment, remittances, and aid to bolster its foreign exchange reserves. The country’s current‑account deficit, driven by high import bills for oil and machinery, has often pressured the rupee downward. In the past, the SBP has employed a range of tools, including market interventions, reserve requirements, and macro‑prudential measures, to manage exchange‑rate volatility. The ongoing global environment, characterized by tighter monetary policy in advanced economies, has added new challenges to an already fragile balance.

What happens next

Economists anticipate that the SBP will continue to use a combination of policy rate adjustments and targeted foreign‑exchange interventions to stabilize the rupee. A possible next step could involve tightening liquidity conditions for banks that hold large foreign‑currency positions, thereby reducing speculative demand. The government may also seek to accelerate export‑promotion initiatives and diversify trade partners to improve the trade balance. Internationally, discussions with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) on a potential financing program could provide additional foreign‑exchange buffers, though such arrangements typically require fiscal consolidation measures. In the short term, market observers expect the rupee to remain volatile, reacting to global commodity price swings and shifts in US monetary policy.
