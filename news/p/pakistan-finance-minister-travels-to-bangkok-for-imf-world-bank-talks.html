Pakistan finance minister travels to Bangkok for IMF, World Bank talks

The finance minister is set to discuss Pakistan's programme with the IMF and World Bank in Bangkok. The talks come as many countries grapple with rising debt burdens.

Latest developments

Finance Minister Shaukat Tarin arrived in Bangkok on Monday to attend a series of meetings with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank. The delegation includes senior officials from the Ministry of Finance and the State Bank of Pakistan. The agenda focuses on reviewing Pakistan's existing IMF programme, assessing possible extensions or adjustments, and discussing new financing options to support the country's balance‑of‑payments needs.

Recent reporting by Dawn highlighted that borrowers of the IMF are increasingly feeling the pressure of debt repayments. Countries that rely on IMF financing are confronting higher interest costs and tighter fiscal targets, which limit their ability to fund social programmes. The article notes that the strain is visible across both emerging and low‑income economies, where debt sustainability assessments are becoming more stringent.

A separate analysis published by Transparency International (TI) warned that almost half of the world’s nations face heightened debt risks. The study points to rising public debt ratios, limited fiscal space, and the potential for debt distress if growth slows. The findings echo concerns raised by the IMF about the need for prudent fiscal management and structural reforms to avoid a wave of defaults.

During the Bangkok talks, Tarin is expected to seek reassurance from the IMF on the timing of the next review of Pakistan’s programme, which is due later this year. He will also engage with World Bank representatives on development projects that could complement macro‑economic stabilization efforts. Observers say that a positive outcome could improve investor confidence and lower borrowing costs, while a muted response may keep pressure on the rupee and inflation.

Why it matters

The IMF programme is a cornerstone of Pakistan’s strategy to stabilise its economy after years of fiscal deficits and a depreciating currency. Access to IMF resources helps the government meet external obligations, maintain foreign exchange reserves, and keep inflation in check. Any shift in the programme’s terms can directly affect the cost of borrowing for businesses and households.

For Pakistani citizens, the outcome of the Bangkok meetings could influence the pace of subsidy reforms, tax adjustments, and public sector wage policies. A smoother agreement may allow the government to preserve social safety nets, whereas stricter conditions could lead to deeper cuts in public spending, affecting health, education and energy subsidies.

Background

Pakistan entered an extended fund facility with the IMF in 2023 after a severe balance‑of‑payments crisis that saw the rupee lose more than half its value against the dollar. The programme, valued at roughly $6 billion, required the country to implement fiscal consolidation, increase tax collection, and reduce the current‑account deficit. Earlier this year, the IMF approved a review that kept the programme on track but highlighted lingering vulnerabilities.

Globally, the IMF has warned that its member countries are navigating a difficult environment marked by high global interest rates, lingering supply‑chain disruptions, and geopolitical tensions. Many borrowers are seeking to refinance existing debt while avoiding new liabilities. The combination of external shocks and internal fiscal challenges has led to a rise in debt‑stress cases, as reflected in the Dawn and TI reports.

What happens next

Following the Bangkok sessions, the finance ministry is expected to release a statement outlining any adjustments to Pakistan’s IMF programme and the status of World Bank project financing. If the IMF grants a favourable review, the government may move forward with a modest increase in its financing ceiling and a revised set of policy targets. Conversely, if disagreements arise, the ministry could seek alternative sources of funding, including bilateral loans or sovereign bonds, to bridge any shortfall.

Summary
