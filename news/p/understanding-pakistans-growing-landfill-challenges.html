Understanding Pakistan’s Growing Landfill Challenges

A major landfill in Pakistan is reaching capacity, prompting public concern and official action. This article explains the current situation, its causes, and what may happen next.

What is happening

In several major cities, especially Karachi and Lahore, municipal waste is being directed to a handful of large landfill sites that are now operating near their designed limits. Recent reports indicate that the primary landfill on the outskirts of Karachi has exceeded its usable height, leading to waste spillage onto nearby roads and residential areas. Local residents have observed increased foul odors, a rise in flies, and occasional fires ignited by exposed waste piles.

The municipal authorities have responded by issuing temporary bans on new waste deliveries to the overloaded sites. Trucks are being rerouted to secondary disposal areas that lack the same level of engineering controls, which has resulted in uneven distribution of waste across the region. This shift has strained the capacity of smaller sites and created logistical challenges for waste collection crews.

Health officials have raised concerns about the potential for water contamination, as runoff from the landfill can seep into groundwater sources used by nearby communities. Respiratory problems have been reported more frequently among residents living close to the sites, and there is a heightened risk of vector‑borne diseases due to the proliferation of rodents and insects.

In response, the federal and provincial governments have announced a series of short‑term measures, including the deployment of additional waste‑handling equipment, the establishment of temporary containment barriers, and the initiation of public awareness campaigns about proper waste segregation. Long‑term plans are said to involve the identification of new landfill locations and the exploration of waste‑to‑energy options, though concrete timelines have not yet been released.

Background and context

Pakistan’s rapid urbanization over the past two decades has outpaced the development of formal waste‑management infrastructure. Many cities rely on a limited number of landfills that were originally designed for smaller populations and lower waste volumes. Over time, informal waste pickers and unregulated dumping have added to the strain on these sites.

Historically, waste collection services have been fragmented, with a mix of municipal departments, private contractors, and community groups handling different parts of the process. This lack of coordination has resulted in inconsistent collection schedules and gaps in service, especially in rapidly expanding neighborhoods. Environmental regulations exist, but enforcement has been uneven, allowing illegal dumping to persist in some areas.

What to know next

Officials have indicated that a comprehensive review of waste‑management practices will be launched within the next month. The review is expected to assess the feasibility of expanding existing landfills, developing new engineered sites, and integrating recycling programs to reduce the volume of waste destined for disposal.

In the meantime, citizens are being encouraged to separate organic waste from recyclables and to avoid disposing of hazardous materials in regular bins. Community groups are organizing clean‑up drives around affected neighborhoods, and some NGOs are piloting composting projects that could provide an alternative outlet for biodegradable waste. These initiatives aim to alleviate immediate pressure on the overburdened landfills while longer‑term solutions are being evaluated.
