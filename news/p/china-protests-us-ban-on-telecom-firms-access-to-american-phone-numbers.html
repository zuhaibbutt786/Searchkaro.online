China protests US ban on telecom firms' access to American phone numbers

Beijing has demanded that Washington rescind its ban on Chinese telecom firms' access to US phone numbers, labeling the move as hostile. The dispute adds another layer to the ongoing US‑China trade friction, with potential implications for

Latest developments

On 22 November 2026, the Chinese foreign ministry issued a statement demanding that the United States cancel the recent restriction that prevents Chinese telecommunications companies from obtaining American phone numbers. Beijing warned that if Washington does not reverse the measure, China will consider "counter‑measures" in the telecommunications sector. The demand was reported by Al Arabiya, which quoted Chinese officials as saying the ban threatens the principle of non‑discrimination in global markets.

Chinese state media echoed the ministry’s position, describing the US decision as "malicious" and "evil" because it limits the ability of firms such as Huawei and ZTE to access US‑based telephone numbering resources. The reports highlighted that the restriction could hamper the companies’ ability to provide services that rely on US numbers, including cloud‑based communication platforms and IoT solutions.

The United States, through the Department of Commerce, has justified the ban on national security grounds, arguing that Chinese telecom equipment poses a risk to critical infrastructure. The policy bars the listed firms from purchasing or leasing US telephone numbers, a step that follows earlier actions that barred the companies from participating in the US 5G rollout. The move is part of a broader strategy to limit Chinese technology influence in sensitive communications networks.

Why it matters

South Africa’s telecom sector relies heavily on equipment and software supplied by Chinese manufacturers. Any disruption to the supply chain could affect the rollout of 5G services, increase costs for network operators, and ultimately raise prices for consumers. Local carriers that partner with Huawei or ZTE may need to seek alternative sources for numbering services, which could delay network upgrades and affect service quality.

The ban also raises data‑security concerns for South African businesses that use Chinese platforms for voice and messaging services. If US phone numbers become unavailable to Chinese providers, companies may be forced to switch to alternative routing arrangements that could be less secure or more expensive. Regulators in South Africa will likely monitor the situation closely to ensure that any supply‑chain adjustments do not compromise the country’s own cybersecurity standards.

Furthermore, the dispute adds another variable to the already complex US‑China trade relationship, which influences foreign‑direct investment flows into the region. South African investors with exposure to Chinese tech firms may see increased volatility, and the broader economic uncertainty could affect decisions on future infrastructure projects.

Background

The United States has maintained a series of restrictions on Chinese technology firms since 2018, citing concerns over espionage and intellectual‑property theft. Earlier measures included bans on Huawei’s participation in 5G networks, limits on ZTE’s access to US components, and a broader export‑control regime targeting advanced semiconductors.

Phone numbers are a critical piece of telecommunications infrastructure because they enable routing of voice calls, SMS, and increasingly, authentication services for online platforms. By denying Chinese firms access to US‑issued numbers, Washington aims to curtail the ability of these companies to offer end‑to‑end communication services that rely on US carriers.

China has repeatedly argued that such restrictions violate World Trade Organization rules and the principle of fair competition. The recent statements from Beijing continue a pattern of diplomatic protests that seek to frame the US actions as politically motivated rather than security‑driven.

What happens next

Diplomatic channels between Beijing and Washington are expected to remain active as both sides assess the economic impact of the ban. Analysts predict that China may introduce reciprocal measures, such as limiting US firms’ access to Chinese numbering resources or imposing additional licensing requirements.

In South Africa, the regulator (ICASA) is likely to issue guidance to local operators on how to mitigate any service disruptions. Telecom companies may also explore partnerships with non‑Chinese vendors to diversify their numbering solutions, while keeping an eye on any further policy shifts from the United States.
