Demolition and 28% Completion of Islamabad Expressway Service Road

The Capital Development Authority cleared more than a hundred illegal structures along the Islamabad Expressway and reported that 28% of the service‑road work is finished. Fifty‑eight buildings have been sealed for operating without permits

Latest developments

The Capital Development Authority (CDA) has cleared more than one hundred illegal structures that had encroached on the right‑hand side of the Islamabad Expressway, according to a Dawn report dated early November 2026. The demolition, carried out over a two‑day operation, targeted shops, residential units and unauthorised commercial stalls that were obstructing the planned widening of the highway.

The CDA also announced that work on the parallel service road is now 28 percent complete, as reported by The News Pakistan. The stretch, which runs alongside the main carriageway, includes new drainage, pedestrian walkways and reinforced pavement designed to improve traffic flow and safety for commuters travelling between the city centre and the new Islamabad‑Rawalpindi ring road.

In a related enforcement action, the CDA sealed 58 buildings that were found to be operating without the required permits, according to The Nation. The sealed premises include several ground‑floor shops that had been using the expressway frontage for storage, as well as a few small factories that were violating zoning regulations.

The demolition and sealing drive forms part of a broader urban renewal programme aimed at de‑congesting the capital’s main traffic artery. Officials say the remaining work on the service road is expected to be finished by mid‑2027, while additional monitoring teams will be deployed to prevent further illegal construction along the corridor.

Why it matters

The expressway is a critical link for commuters travelling between Islamabad, the federal capital, and the industrial zones of Rawalpindi. By removing illegal encroachments and advancing the service‑road project, traffic congestion is expected to drop, reducing travel times that currently exceed one hour during peak periods.

Improved traffic flow also has economic implications. Faster movement of goods and services along the corridor can lower logistics costs for businesses, potentially attracting new investment to the region and supporting the government’s broader goal of modernising Pakistan’s transport infrastructure.

The removal of encroachments also enhances road safety by eliminating blind spots and illegal parking zones that have been the cause of frequent minor accidents. Residents near the demolition sites have been urged to relocate temporarily, with compensation packages under discussion.

Background

The Islamabad Expressway, completed in the early 2000s, was originally designed as a six‑lane arterial road to connect the capital with the twin city of Rawalpindi. Over the years, rapid urbanisation and lax enforcement led to the proliferation of unauthorised structures along its median and service lanes, compromising safety and capacity.

In response, the CDA launched a series of enforcement campaigns beginning in 2024, targeting illegal shops, residential units and factories that had sprung up without proper permits. The latest phase, reported by multiple Pakistani news outlets, focuses on the expressway’s right‑hand side, where the majority of the illegal encroachments were concentrated.

What happens next

The CDA has set a target to finish the remaining 72 percent of the service‑road work by June 2027, with regular progress updates planned for public release. Additional surveillance units will be stationed along the expressway to enforce zoning rules and prevent new illegal constructions.

If the project stays on schedule, commuters could see a reduction of up to 20 percent in travel time during rush hour, while businesses along the corridor may benefit from a cleaner, more accessible environment.
