Pakistan Finance Minister Announces Energy Import Strategy Amid Winter Demand

Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb warned of a possible winter energy shortfall and outlined a plan to import crude from Libya. The move aims to keep power flowing and prices stable for households and businesses.

Latest developments

Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb told the parliament that Pakistan's current energy reserves are sufficient to meet demand until the end of November, but a sharp rise in consumption during the winter months could test the system. He stressed the urgency of securing additional crude oil supplies to avoid any interruption in power generation.

The ministry has entered negotiations with Libya to import crude oil, a step intended to diversify Pakistan's supply sources that have traditionally relied on a narrow group of exporters. Officials indicated that the prospective agreement could bring as much as 30,000 barrels per day, which would help stabilise domestic fuel prices and reduce pressure on the foreign exchange market.

Energy analysts point out that Libya's oil production has rebounded after years of conflict, reaching roughly 1.2 million barrels per day. This recovery makes Libyan crude an attractive option for countries like Pakistan that are looking to broaden their import basket. The talks are also seen as part of a wider economic outreach that could open doors to further trade and investment between the two nations.

In parallel, the finance ministry announced a modest increase in the energy budget to fund storage facilities and distribution logistics ahead of the colder season. The additional allocation is aimed at covering transportation costs, ensuring that power plants receive a steady feedstock supply, and maintaining strategic reserves.

Opposition lawmakers warned that greater reliance on imported crude could widen the trade deficit, but Aurangzeb argued that the economic cost of a winter power shortfall would be far higher. He called on the parliament to approve the necessary foreign‑exchange allocation without delay to enable timely procurement.

Why it matters

For millions of Pakistani households, a reliable energy supply during winter means fewer load‑shedding outages, which directly affects daily life, education and health services. Stable electricity also supports small businesses that depend on uninterrupted power to operate.

From an economic perspective, securing a steady flow of crude oil helps keep transport and manufacturing costs predictable. This predictability is crucial for controlling inflation, which has been a persistent concern for policymakers and consumers alike.

The decision to engage with Libya signals Pakistan's intent to broaden its diplomatic and commercial ties beyond traditional partners. A successful oil deal could pave the way for additional cooperation in sectors such as construction, agriculture and tourism, potentially attracting new foreign investment.

Background

Pakistan has struggled with chronic energy deficits for more than a decade, with load‑shedding becoming a recurring political issue. Past attempts to import oil were often hampered by limited foreign‑exchange reserves and volatile global oil prices, leading to delayed shipments and price spikes.

Libya, after a protracted civil war, has revived its oil sector and is now producing around 1.2 million barrels per day. With European demand fluctuating, Libya is actively seeking new markets to sustain its export revenues, making Pakistan a potential partner.

The finance ministry, under Aurangzeb's leadership, has previously negotiated fuel agreements with Saudi Arabia and Iran. These past deals illustrate a pattern of seeking diversified sources to reduce dependence on any single supplier and to improve bargaining power in the global oil market.

What happens next

The two governments are expected to sign a memorandum of understanding within the next month. If the agreement is finalized, the first shipments of Libyan crude could begin by early 2025, subject to payment terms and logistical arrangements.

Parliament will soon debate the proposed foreign‑exchange allocation, and the energy ministry will continue to monitor winter demand trends. Adjustments to storage plans and budgetary provisions will be made based on real‑time consumption data.
